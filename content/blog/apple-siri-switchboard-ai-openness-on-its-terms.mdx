# Apple Is Building an AI Switchboard While Arguing Against Openness on Android

Judging by the reporting, Apple spent the first half of May opening up. [Bloomberg reported on May 5](https://9to5mac.com/2026/05/05/ios-27-will-let-you-choose-between-gemini-claude-and-more-for-ai-features-report/) that iOS 27 would let people choose between Gemini, Claude, and more for AI features. On May 13 Reuters reported that Apple had [warned the European Commission against making Google give rival AI services deeper access to Android](https://9to5mac.com/2026/05/13/apple-warns-eu-against-forcing-google-to-open-android-to-ai-rivals/), citing what it called profound risks to privacy, security, and safety, and on May 14 Bloomberg reported that OpenAI was weighing a breach-of-contract notice over ChatGPT in Siri, which [TechCrunch framed](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/14/openai-is-reportedly-preparing-legal-action-against-apple-it-wouldnt-be-the-first-partner-to-feel-burned/) as one more partner feeling burned by Apple. What I see underneath them is Apple building a switchboard that decides which outside models and agents reach iPhone users and on what terms. I'd argue the switchboard is the right product design, and the Brussels filing is where Apple's position gets weak, because it defends openness where Apple writes the rules and argues against it where a regulator would write them.

The May reports built on [Bloomberg's March 26 report](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-26/apple-plans-to-open-up-siri-to-rival-ai-assistants-beyond-chatgpt-in-ios-27) of an Extensions system for iOS 27 that would let AI assistants beyond ChatGPT plug into Siri, with a new App Store section for them. The May 5 report named Siri, Writing Tools, and Image Playground as the features, and on May 12 Bloomberg described [a rebuilt Siri](https://9to5mac.com/2026/05/12/ios-27s-completely-rebuilt-siri-will-include-a-new-system-wide-search-gesture-report/) that takes actions across apps and has a search bar that can switch from Siri to ChatGPT or Gemini as the search engine. Apple hasn't confirmed any of the iOS 27 reports, and in early May it agreed to pay $250 million, admitting no wrongdoing, to settle claims that it promoted a personal Siri that wasn't available, so I'd treat every reported Siri feature as a plan until it ships.

With that caveat, I think model choice is the right product. I don't think any one model is best at everything people ask a phone to do, and Apple has already accepted that the best model for a job can come from someone else, since its own next generation of foundation models will be based on Google's Gemini models, under a collaboration the two companies announced on January 12. I think letting people pick would keep them starting in Siri even when another company's model does the work, which answers the worry I raised in December, that [Apple Music inside ChatGPT](/writing/apple-music-in-chatgpt-assistant-entry-point) gave people one more reason to start somewhere else. It's also close to the lesson I argued in February that Apple should carry over from [letting rival coding agents into Xcode](/writing/apple-xcode-rival-coding-agents-developer-bet), owning the place where the work happens and letting other companies supply the intelligence.

The agents report is the part I'd defend most. [The Information reported on May 13](https://9to5mac.com/2026/05/13/apple-is-working-to-incorporate-ai-agents-on-the-app-store-per-report/) that Apple is building a framework to admit AI agents that act for users inside the App Store's privacy and security rules. An agent that reads an email and then acts inside other apps is the setup I wrote about on May 6, when I argued that [prompt injection is a product problem](/writing/prompt-injection-is-a-product-problem) because the defenses that work are decisions about what an agent may touch. Deciding what an agent can do inside someone else's app is that kind of decision, and on the iPhone, Apple is the obvious company to make it.

The Information also said part of Apple's motive is keeping agents from routing around App Store fees, and the same report said Apple is courting developers in China, including Baidu, Alibaba, and Tencent, to wire their apps into the new Siri, which [9to5Mac took to mean](https://9to5mac.com/2026/05/13/apple-wants-apps-to-integrate-with-siri-in-ios-27-but-one-fear-holds-some-back-report/) App Intents, the framework apps use to expose their actions to Siri. Some of them are hesitating, because Apple told them it charges no commission at the start but hasn't ruled out charging one later. I think those developers are reading it correctly, because the company that runs the review also decides privately whether to charge, and can change that answer after developers have built on Siri.

Apple's filing in Brussels is harder to square with all of this. Under the Digital Markets Act, the EU law that sets obligations for the largest platforms, the Commission's draft measures would give rival AI services deeper access to Android so they could act inside installed apps, for tasks like sending an email or ordering food. Beyond the privacy and security risks, Apple faulted the Commission for redesigning Android after less than three months of work. An assistant acting inside other apps is close to what Bloomberg says the new Siri will do, and to what Apple wants developers to enable by integrating with the new Siri, and I'd guess Apple worries that rules written for Android could become a template for the iPhone.

Apple does have a real defense here, which is that it's being consistent and the stakes are real. It made the same privacy and security argument about the DMA's obligations on its own platform before the EU's General Court in October 2025, and an AI service acting inside other apps deserves strict gatekeeping. A choice of several outside models would also open Siri further than Apple ever has, because today ChatGPT is the only outside chatbot Siri can hand a request to. I'd concede all of that. But the security argument proves too much, because taken to its end it says only the platform owner may decide which assistants get deep access to people's apps, which is the App Store's gatekeeper position rebuilt for AI. I still think Apple should review agents on the iPhone, and what I don't accept is that only Apple may write the criteria, since review works as well against published rules, and criteria set privately can be changed privately.

OpenAI is already running into terms Apple sets privately. [Bloomberg reported on May 14](https://9to5mac.com/2026/05/14/openai-preparing-legal-action-against-apple-over-siri-partnership-report/) that OpenAI had expected deeper ChatGPT integration across Apple's apps, more prominent placement in Siri, and more promotion. OpenAI isn't a neutral witness, since a reported grievance is also negotiating pressure, and no response from Apple was reported. Part of the complaint is that the billions a year in subscriptions OpenAI expected from the partnership hasn't come close to happening, which could be a product problem as much as a placement one, but the part that matters here is that integration, placement, and promotion are Apple's to grant or hold back, and Extensions as reported would put Google, Anthropic, and every other model provider in the same position.

May's moves look to me like Apple rebuilding the App Store for AI, from a storefront section for assistants, to review rules for agents, to a fee question left open, and I think the product underneath is good for iPhone users. What would change things for partners is Apple publishing the switchboard's rules before WWDC, meaning which assistants qualify for Extensions, what an agent may do inside another company's app, and whether Siri integrations will ever carry a fee. Model providers would know what placement to expect before signing on, which is where, according to Bloomberg's reporting, OpenAI thinks Apple fell short, and developers like the Chinese companies Apple is courting could decide whether to build on Siri knowing the terms. Apple's case in Brussels would be easier to believe too, because a company asking regulators to trust its judgment on deep access should let partners check that judgment against rules they can read.
