# By Merging Its Copilot Apps, Microsoft Decided What Consumer Copilot Is For

When Microsoft began merging its consumer Copilot app and the Microsoft 365 Copilot app into one app on August 13, TechCrunch's Sarah Perez [wrote that Copilot "has lost its way,"](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/13/microsoft-kills-off-unsuccessful-ai-features-while-merging-its-separate-copilot-apps/) and read the merger as an acknowledgment that Microsoft's old setup was too complicated to make Copilot a viable competitor to ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. The Register covered what Microsoft was giving up, from Mico, the animated character in Copilot's voice mode, being quieted less than a year after its debut, to an apology as Microsoft said it would retire a preview formula in Excel. My take is that Microsoft finally decided what consumer Copilot is for. The merger makes Copilot the front door to the Microsoft 365 subscription, which is where Microsoft's consumer advantage actually is, and I think it's the most honest consumer AI decision the company has made.

## One app with Microsoft 365 behind it

The app Microsoft began rolling out on August 13, with mobile and web in the first wave according to Windows Central, puts the Copilot chatbot and its image generation [alongside the Microsoft 365 apps, files, and other content](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/13/microsofts-dueling-copilot-apps-have-combined-into-a-single-entity/5287512). Free users can keep chatting, making images, and uploading files subject to capacity limits, and Microsoft's notice tells anyone who wants higher limits, agents, or complex multi-step tasks to buy a Microsoft 365 subscription. TechCrunch tied the consolidation to an internal memo, reported by The Information in July, in which Jacob Andreou, who runs Copilot, said the app had to earn "the right to exist" in customers' lives by moving on from features that didn't work. In March I wrote that Microsoft [started fixing Copilot by taking things away](/writing/microsoft-copilot-one-owner-fewer-entry-points) when Satya Nadella made Andreou its single owner across consumer and commercial, and the merged app carries that change into the product.

I think one app is the right shape, because it attaches Copilot to what Microsoft already has with consumers. Nadella told investors on April 29 that consumer Microsoft 365 had [nearly 95 million subscribers](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/events/fy-2026/earnings-fy-2026-q3), the count Amy Hood put at more than 90 million subscriptions in October 2025, and since a Family plan covers up to six people, I read it as paying accounts. It says nothing about Copilot use, but it is the paying base Microsoft already has, and in the merged app Copilot sits next to the documents and files those subscribers open anyway.

## What got cut, and what it costs

The cuts point the same way. Group Chat, Podcasts, and consumer Deep Research were scheduled to retire on August 18 along with Copilot Labs' experiments, and The Register reported that group chats and everything in them would be wiped. Of those four consumer retirements, Deep Research is the only one the coverage pairs with a replacement, and it fits my case, though it would also fit a plain cleanup of two overlapping features. Deep Research leaves the consumer Copilot app, and Microsoft 365 Premium subscribers get Researcher, a Microsoft 365 agent for similar research and report writing, in its place. What that means is that this kind of research becomes part of what people pay Premium for. Mico, which [launched on by default in Copilot's voice mode](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/23/microsofts-mico-is-a-clippy-for-the-ai-era/) on October 23, 2025, is [coming out of Copilot Voice](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/14/less-than-a-year-on-microsoft-tells-mico-to-pipe-down/5287793) and moving to Learn Live, a voice-based study mode, The Register reported on August 14. TechCrunch's headline called the retired features unsuccessful, but none of the coverage I read cited usage figures, so the cut itself is the only evidence that they failed, and it fits a change in what Copilot is for just as well.

I'd call the cuts honest, because a group chat, an AI-generated podcast, and an animated character are what a destination app adds to give people a reason to open it before ChatGPT, and once Copilot's job is pulling people further into the Microsoft 365 apps they pay for, those features stop having a job. That is a different test from the one Andreou's memo named, which was moving on from features that didn't work, though the two tests lead to the same cuts.

The cost is real, and it lands on the people Microsoft will need first next time. On August 17 The Register reported that Microsoft plans to retire Excel's [preview COPILOT() function](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/17/excels-copilot-function-is-headed-for-the-recycle-bin/5288327) on September 14, a formula Beta Channel users with Microsoft 365 Copilot licenses have had since August 2025, and that Microsoft apologized, said it won't ship the feature, and pointed people to the Copilot side pane. Still, I'd guess that anyone who built formulas on COPILOT() under a business license, kept a group chat going, or got used to Mico takes away that early adopters pay when a Copilot feature goes away, and that waiting is the sensible response. I think the trade is still worth it, since an app that keeps every experiment alive gets harder to explain with each one, but Microsoft used up some early adopters' patience in August, and it will need that patience the next time it asks people to try something early.

## Who a front door leaves out

The best case against my read is that a front door only serves people who are already inside. If agents, multi-step tasks, and Researcher sit behind a Microsoft 365 plan, consumer Copilot's best features stop at the subscriber base, and the habit that may prove the most durable position in consumer AI, being the app people open first with a question, is left to ChatGPT. The merger also ties consumer AI more tightly to a paid plan, the same link Italy's competition authority [opened a case over](https://en.agcm.it/en/media/press-releases/2026/6/PS13129) on June 26, alleging that Microsoft added Copilot and Designer to consumer Microsoft 365 subscriptions without clear information and moved people onto a higher-priced plan by default unless they withdrew. I laid out in October 2025 [what I think that kind of bundle owes customers](/writing/microsoft-consumer-copilot-bundle-visible-exit), and routing more of Copilot through the paid plan makes that question matter more.

The reach argument is right about what Microsoft gave up, but I think it overstates how much there was to give up. The free tier still covers chat, images, and file uploads within capacity limits, so the cap falls on the heavier work. As a standalone app, consumer Copilot was asking people to move their first-open habit away from a product that had it first. Microsoft's advantage sits in the subscription, and the merger puts Copilot's effort there. I'd be wrong if ChatGPT became the place people draft documents and keep files too, since subscribers would then have little reason to open Copilot at all, and August doesn't settle that.

Not quite ten months after Mico arrived, the conclusion I'd draw is that Microsoft's consumer AI makes the most sense when it admits what it is attached to. Microsoft said Mico taught it about warmth, expressiveness, and how people want to talk with AI, and I'd argue a character like that is what a standalone brand builds when it needs people to choose it over ChatGPT. In the same week Microsoft began moving Mico out of voice, it began putting Copilot beside the Microsoft 365 apps and people's files, so the product says consumer Copilot is attached to the subscription. Whether that attachment is worth much depends on whether subscribers who find Copilot beside their documents start using it for work they would have taken elsewhere, and that's the number I'd most like Microsoft to publish.
