# Apple's Lawsuit Against OpenAI Is Aimed at the Next Hire as Much as the Two It Names

The lawsuit Apple filed against OpenAI on July 10 is easy to take at face value, as a trade secret case about two former employees and a set of confidential hardware files. I read it as something larger. It's about Chang Liu and Tang Tan, and I think it's just as much about whoever OpenAI tries to hire out of Apple's hardware teams next, because Apple has been losing hardware people to OpenAI since at least the fall of 2025, has already tried paying some of its iPhone hardware designers to stay, and has now reached for a tool that raises the cost of both leaving and hiring.

The [complaint](https://9to5mac.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/2026/07/Apple-Inc.-v.-Liu-et-al.pdf), filed in federal court in San Jose, names Liu, a former senior system electrical engineer who left Apple in January, and Tan, Apple's former vice president of iPhone and Apple Watch product design and now OpenAI's chief hardware officer, along with OpenAI and io Products, the hardware startup Jony Ive co-founded and OpenAI bought for $6.5 billion. Apple alleges that Liu kept an Apple laptop and used a former colleague's authenticated Apple computer, along with a previously unknown authentication bug, to download dozens of confidential hardware files, and that Tan used Apple project code names in interviews and had candidates bring actual parts to show-and-tell sessions. Every allegation is unproven, and OpenAI told Bloomberg on July 14 that it is ["not aware of any evidence that this complaint has merit."](https://9to5mac.com/2026/07/14/openai-says-it-has-seen-no-evidence-supporting-apples-trade-secret-theft-claims/)

The sequence before the filing is why I read the suit as a retention decision too. On November 17, 2025, Bloomberg reported that iPhone Air designer Abidur Chowdhury had left for an AI startup, [after a run of Apple designers, from Evans Hankey to Tang Tan, had already gone to Jony Ive's io, which OpenAI bought](https://9to5mac.com/2025/11/17/iphone-air-designer-leaves-apple-for-ai-startup/). On March 26 Bloomberg reported that Apple had given iPhone hardware designers [rare out-of-cycle stock grants worth roughly $200,000 to $400,000 over four years of vesting](https://9to5mac.com/2026/03/26/apple-gives-rare-bonuses-to-iphone-designers-to-deter-departures/) after recruiting by OpenAI and startups picked up, and the same report said OpenAI's hardware group had hired several dozen Apple engineers. When Apple named its hardware engineering chief, John Ternus, as its next CEO on April 20, Bloomberg framed keeping top talent as a test for him. In April I argued that the succession [divides Apple's top job in two, with an engineer running the products and Tim Cook helping handle policymakers](/writing/apple-cook-executive-chairman-policy-role), and the departures are coming out of the hardware organization that the same succession handed to Johny Srouji, Apple's chip lead, when he took Ternus's job as chief hardware officer. Then on June 26 Bloomberg reported that [Paul Meade, at Apple since 2010 and the head of its Vision Products Group, which builds Vision Pro and Apple's planned smart glasses, was leaving for OpenAI](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/06/26/apple-loses-another-executive-to-openai/), and MacRumors tied his exit to the executive changes around the succession, saying Srouji's move into the chief hardware officer role had upset some hardware executives. So Meade is at least as much a story about Apple's own reorganization as about OpenAI's offers. The grants also went to iPhone designers and not to Meade's group, so I can't say the money failed with the people who actually got it.

If Apple's evidence holds, suing is the correct response, because a company that believes an engineer walked out with confidential hardware files should act, and trade secret cases tend to rise or fall on specifics like the laptop and the bug. But the complaint reaches well past what Liu allegedly took. It makes OpenAI's hiring part of the case, from the code names in interviews, to the parts candidates were told to bring, to Apple's count that more than 400 former Apple employees now work at OpenAI. Some of that number is probably the ordinary flow of people from a very large employer to a fast-growing one, but Apple chose to put it in a trade secret complaint. A week after the filing, the Financial Times reported that Apple had sent [about 40 evidence-preservation letters](https://9to5mac.com/2026/07/17/apple-sends-legal-letters-to-former-employees-now-at-openai/) to former employees now at OpenAI. Preservation letters are routine once a suit is filed, and they also tell everyone who gets one that Apple is paying attention. What I think that means is that an Apple engineer weighing an offer from OpenAI, and the OpenAI interviewer deciding what to ask that engineer, both now have a reason to be more careful, which I'd argue is much of the point.

There's a more charitable reading. Apple says in the complaint that it raised its concerns with OpenAI in February and got no response, though NBC News reported on July 15 that OpenAI says it did respond and that the talks broke down after an outside lawyer for Apple emailed the wrong OpenAI person. Either way the suit was months in the making, and a company that believes files were taken has little choice but to sue. A public fight can also backfire by making Apple look like an employer that punishes people for leaving, which could hurt its own recruiting. I accept the first point, and I think a legitimate property claim and a deterrent effect can live in the same filing. A court will decide the claim, but any deterrent effect reaches every hardware engineer who reads about the case in the meantime, and that's the part I think is strategic. The second point is a real risk, and my guess is that Apple judged it smaller than the risk of letting the departures continue without an answer.

Where Apple chose to fight shows which part of its business it thinks it can least afford to lose to a rival. At the model layer it has been willing to work with outsiders. On January 12 Apple and Google said [the next generation of Apple Foundation Models will be based on Google's Gemini models and cloud technology](https://blog.google/company-news/inside-google/company-announcements/joint-statement-google-apple/), and ChatGPT is already built into Siri, a partnership that had frayed by May 14, when Bloomberg reported that [OpenAI was weighing a breach-of-contract notice against Apple over the ChatGPT integration in Siri](https://9to5mac.com/2026/05/14/openai-preparing-legal-action-against-apple-over-siri-partnership-report/). Apple put up with an unhappy partner at the model layer and went to court over device engineering. I read the suit as Apple treating OpenAI's device work as a real threat to the layer it most wants to keep in-house, the design and engineering of its devices.

The limit of a lawsuit as a retention tool is that it raises the cost of leaving Apple without doing anything to make staying at Apple more attractive. It does nothing for the engineer deciding whether four more years at Apple are worth more than a role on OpenAI's hardware team. That second job is the one the March grants were built for, since stock that vests over four years pays people only for staying. In July 2025 I argued that the AI talent war's [nine-figure offers were the clearest signal of where the scarce input in AI sits, in a small number of people and a lot of compute](/writing/ai-talent-war-summer-2025). A year later the scarce people Apple is fighting over are the ones who know how to ship hardware at Apple's scale, and I think the grants, and the work itself, will have to do more to keep them than any courtroom can.
