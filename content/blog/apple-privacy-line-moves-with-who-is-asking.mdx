# Apple's Privacy Promise Bends Depending on Who Is Asking

Apple had four stories in October that tested its privacy pitch, and the coverage mostly handled them one at a time. Fox Business wrote up the October 2 removal of ICEBlock as Apple acting after pressure from Attorney General Pam Bondi, Apple's own October 8 notice about the Texas age rules was written for developers, the bigger bug bounty on October 10 read as a security story, and 9to5Mac's October 23 write-up framed Apple's warning about App Tracking Transparency as Apple decrying intense lobbying in Europe. I think they're one story about where Apple's privacy promise, the pitch that it's on the user's side, stops. What I'd argue iPhone users actually buy is some ability to predict when Apple will fight on their behalf, and October showed that line holding firm against anyone attacking the phone through its code and moving depending on which government was asking.

The bounty is where the promise is strongest. On October 10 Apple [doubled its top Security Bounty award to $2 million](https://security.apple.com/blog/apple-security-bounty-evolved/) for exploit chains that can do what mercenary spyware does, where an exploit chain is a series of bugs linked together to take over a phone. Bonuses for things like bypassing Lockdown Mode, the extreme protection setting Apple built for people who might be targeted, can push a single payout past $5 million. Apple tied the redesign to Memory Integrity Enforcement, the memory safety protection it built into every iPhone 17 model and iPhone Air, which fits what I wrote in September about [Apple executing at the top of its game on hardware](/writing/apple-iphone-17-ai-strategy-2025). What's interesting here is the adversary. Apple's post describes the attacks this bounty targets as historically associated with state actors and aimed at a very small number of people, so the adversary is often a government, or someone working for one, that comes in through the code with no legal standing, into hardware and software Apple controls end to end.

ICEBlock shows the line moving. On the evening of October 2 Apple [removed ICEBlock](https://www.foxbusiness.com/politics/apple-takes-down-ice-tracking-app-after-pressure-from-ag-bondi), an app for anonymously reporting sightings of Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents. Bondi said the Justice Department had reached out to Apple that day demanding the removal, saying the app was designed to put ICE agents at risk, and Apple said it acted on information from law enforcement about safety risks tied to the app. I'm taking both reasons at face value, since I don't know what law enforcement showed Apple and this isn't an argument about immigration enforcement. What bothers me is how little of the process anyone can see. Neither statement pointed to a statute or a court order, and the app was gone the same day the demand came in, so from the outside there's no way to tell what standard Apple applied.

Apple does publish an annual [App Store Transparency Report](https://www.apple.com/legal/more-resources/docs/2024-App-Store-Transparency-Report.pdf) that counts apps removed on government takedown demands by country, with the government entity and the law cited in a supplemental file. The edition covering 2024, published in May, lists removals in mainland China, Russia, South Korea, and eleven other countries and regions, and none in the United States. It doesn't say how Apple decides whether to comply, or how it tells a government demand apart from its own call after hearing from law enforcement. Maybe removal was the right call on what Apple knew, and maybe refusing would have started a fight Apple couldn't win. But an invisible process is a product failure for a company that sells trust, and the most useful thing Apple could add to that report is the test it applies before it says yes.

Texas and Europe are where Apple objected in public and still treated the local law as the boundary. On October 8 Apple [told developers](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=btkirlj8) that from January 1, under Texas's SB2420, anyone in Texas creating a new Apple Account will have to confirm whether they're 18 or older, accounts for anyone under 18 will have to join a Family Sharing group, and a parent or guardian will have to approve downloads and purchases. Apple said it shares the goal of protecting kids but is concerned the law requires collecting sensitive personal information from anyone who wants to download any app, and it said Utah and Louisiana have similar requirements coming later in 2026. I think that objection is fair, but objecting and then building it anyway means what an iPhone asks of a new user will depend on where they live.

Europe runs the same pattern. On October 21, at an EU General Court hearing in Luxembourg that Bloomberg called [the Digital Markets Act's biggest court test](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-21/apple-attacks-eu-crackdown-in-digital-law-s-biggest-court-test), Apple's lawyer Daniel Beard argued that the act, the EU's competition law for the largest platforms, puts hugely onerous and intrusive burdens on Apple and overlooks privacy and security. The next day Apple told the German Press Agency that intense lobbying in Germany, Italy, and other European countries [may force it to withdraw App Tracking Transparency](https://9to5mac.com/2025/10/23/apple-decries-intense-lobbying-threatens-to-turn-off-app-tracking-transparency-in-europe/) for European users. ATT is the prompt that asks whether an app can track you across other companies' apps and websites, and Germany's competition regulator has been examining whether it's anticompetitive. I'd concede the warning is probably as much pressure on regulators as it is a plan, and that Apple is fighting to keep the feature. But whether a European user keeps that prompt depends on a negotiation they can't see.

The strongest objection to my reading is that one rule runs under all of this, which is that Apple follows the law where it sells and argues in public when it disagrees. Apple's transparency report states the principle itself, saying apps must follow the law in each country or region where they operate and that an app removed on a government demand comes down only where that government has jurisdiction and stays available everywhere else. Saying no in any of these cases could expose the company, its employees, and in some countries its users to worse outcomes. I accept that, and it's actually my point. If the law of each place is the boundary, then Apple's privacy guarantee is a set of local guarantees, and ICEBlock shows that even a local one can come down to Apple's private reading of a request.

Apple's privacy promise, as October showed it, comes in two strengths. The technical strength covers anyone trying to get into an iPhone through its software, including the spyware Apple associates with state actors, and there a user can count on Apple putting real money and engineering on their side, because Apple controls that whole fight. The political strength covers what happens when a government asks through official channels, and there a user can count on less, since Apple will follow the law wherever they are, and anything past that, from objecting in public, to taking the argument to court as it did in Luxembourg, depends on the case. The first is a promise I'd rely on, and the second is only as strong as the law where you live and Apple's reading of whoever is asking.
