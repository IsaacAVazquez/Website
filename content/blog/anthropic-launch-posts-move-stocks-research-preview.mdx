# When Investors Read Anthropic's Blog, a Research Preview Label Has to Mean Something

Three times in February, other companies' stocks fell sharply within days of something Anthropic published, first legal and data companies, then cybersecurity companies, and then IBM, and Reuters and the analysts it quoted treated each fall as a verdict on the incumbents. When Thomson Reuters fell nearly 18 percent on February 3, on track for its biggest daily loss on record, [Reuters quoted Schroders' Jonathan McMullan](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/anthropics-ai-tools-deepen-selloff-054551832.html) saying the selling reflected a deepening structural debate about incumbents that Anthropic's legal tool had accelerated. I read the month differently. Two of the three were research previews and the third was an argument on a company blog, and I think at least some of the selling treated the previews as finished products and the argument as a demonstrated result, though the same drops also fit a market pricing where these tools are headed, and the price moves alone can't separate the two. Anthropic still owns part of this, because once investors start reading a company's launch posts as news about other companies, the way it writes them becomes a strategic decision, and a label like research preview has to tell readers who has access, what is missing, and what exactly is being claimed.

The plugins set the pattern. On January 30 Anthropic [added plugins to Cowork](https://claude.com/blog/cowork-plugins), its agent for general knowledge work, and open-sourced 11 of them covering work like sales, finance, data, and legal, each one a bundle of editable files. The post called plugin support a research preview for all paid Claude users, told readers to start from the open-source set and customize it, and said plugins were saved locally on each person's machine, with org-wide sharing still weeks away. My verdict is that to the extent the selling was about this post, it priced a preview as a product, because a folder of files on one person's machine is a starting point for a workflow. The post left a gap, though, because a reader looking for what the legal plugin could actually do got a single line saying it reviews documents, flags risks, and tracks compliance, and nothing I could find about how well it does any of the three.

The second case shows that a clear label only goes so far. On February 20 Anthropic launched [Claude Code Security](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-code-security), which scans codebases for security flaws and proposes patches, and here the availability was about as clear as a launch post gets, a limited research preview for Enterprise and Team customers, faster access for open-source maintainers, and nothing applied without a person's approval. That same day [Investing.com reported](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/cybersecurity-stocks-drop-anthropic-launches-203607452.html) CrowdStrike down 6.8 percent, Okta down 9.2 percent, and Cloudflare down 6.7 percent, and tied the drop to fear that AI agents would eat into threat-detection subscriptions. With the same condition, the verdict is close to the one for the plugins, but the lesson is different, because this time the post said plainly who could use the product and the selling happened anyway. The post also said Anthropic's team had used Opus 4.6 to find more than 500 vulnerabilities in production open-source code. What I think this means is that a launch post's biggest claim sets expectations whatever its access terms say.

The COBOL post is where Anthropic's writing choices mattered most, because it was only an argument. On February 23 the Claude blog argued that Claude Code can automate the exploration and analysis work it called the most expensive step in modernizing decades-old COBOL systems, so that [teams can modernize in quarters instead of years](https://claude.com/blog/how-ai-helps-break-cost-barrier-cobol-modernization). The rest of the post was more careful, but its conditions, that engineers keep the judgment on regulation, business priorities, and risk and that the work moves in small validated steps, came later, in sections of their own. That day IBM fell about 13 percent, its worst day since October 2000, according to [CIO](https://www.cio.com/article/4137185/anthropics-claim-that-ai-can-quickly-refactor-cobol-rattles-ibm-investors.html), which also carried IBM software chief Rob Thomas's rebuttal that translating the code is the small part of the job. My verdict is harsher here. In early 2025 that line would have read as a vendor's pitch, but this one came from a company that [reported a $14 billion annualized run-rate on February 12](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-raises-30-billion-series-g-funding-380-billion-post-money-valuation), with Claude Code's own run-rate above $2.5 billion, and I think a claim from a company that size needs its conditions in the same sentence, so no one can quote one without the other.

Anthropic could fairly answer that none of this is its job. On that view, Anthropic isn't responsible for how traders read a blog post, since the debate about what AI does to software and information companies was already deepening when the plugins arrived, which McMullan's own wording concedes, and asking a lab to soften its launch writing to protect other companies' share prices would be a strange kind of self-censorship. The causal link is contested too, because plenty of other things move a stock in a given week. I'd grant all of that, and since I set up my investing year on the assumption that [I can't forecast it](/writing/2026-investing-setup-rates-and-risk), I'm not claiming to know where any of these stocks should trade. My argument rests only on who reads the posts. The Financial Times reported in December that Anthropic had hired Wilson Sonsini for a possible IPO as early as 2026, and a company in that position should expect investors to read its launch posts the way they read disclosures.

Anthropic's own February 24 briefing on enterprise agents, at least as TechCrunch reported it, came closer to the better version. It [added a private plugin marketplace with admin controls](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/24/anthropic-launches-new-push-for-enterprise-agents-with-plugins-for-finance-engineering-and-design/), department plugins, and a way for companies to build their own, which is the org-wide management the January 30 post had said was coming. TechCrunch also quoted Kate Jensen, Anthropic's head of Americas, saying that "2025 was meant to be the year agents transformed the enterprise, but the hype turned out to be mostly premature," which is the tone I wish the COBOL post had taken. The briefing comes close to the one list I'd hold a launch post to. Its first few sentences should say who can use the product and on which plans, how mature it is and what the preview still lacks, and what exactly the company claims it can do and under what conditions, since those first sentences are the part that gets quoted.

The audience for an Anthropic launch post changed in February. The harness block in January, where I argued Anthropic [drew the right line on subscriptions and enforced it the wrong way](/writing/anthropic-subscription-harness-block-developer-trust) by blocking third-party harnesses overnight with no notice that the existing terms would now be enforced, was a problem of writing too little. February's posts had a different problem, since they were written for developers and customers and then read by people deciding what legal publishers, security vendors, and IBM are worth, and, if the IPO reporting is right, eventually by public investors deciding what Anthropic itself is worth. What I'd pull out of February is that writing for all of those readers asks less of Anthropic than it sounds, because it can promise as much as it did in February as long as the opening paragraph would still be accurate if a trader who has never opened Claude Code quoted it alone.
