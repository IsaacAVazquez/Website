# Microsoft's OpenAI Exclusivity Now Covers the Part of the Stack That Matters Least

On February 27 Microsoft and OpenAI put out a [joint statement saying nothing in OpenAI's new deals changed their terms](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/02/27/microsoft-and-openai-joint-statement-on-continuing-partnership/), and the statement itself said it wanted the new deals understood within the existing construct of the partnership. February's coverage kept asking whether Microsoft was losing OpenAI, from a February 4 Bloomberg headline calling the partnership more risk than reward, to a February 12 Windows Central headline that turned Mustafa Suleyman's Financial Times interview about [AI self-sufficiency with Microsoft's own frontier models](https://www.ft.com/content/f1ec830c-2f08-4b1a-b70f-7330f260753c) into a plan to ditch OpenAI, which goes further than the remarks the FT reported. On February 27 OpenAI also announced [$110 billion in new funding](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/27/openai-raises-110b-in-one-of-the-largest-private-funding-rounds-in-history/) at a $730 billion pre-money valuation, including $50 billion from Amazon, $35 billion of it conditional. The question I'd ask instead is what Microsoft's cloud exclusivity actually covers after February 27. Reading the statement next to Amazon's announcement, my answer is the stateless model call, which I'd argue is also the part of OpenAI's business a customer can most easily take to another model provider, since a single request and response, with nothing kept afterward, carries nothing that has to move with it. The stateful runtime that agents would depend on, meaning memory, identity, and access to tools, is being built with Amazon on Bedrock, and Frontier, OpenAI's own agent platform, stays hosted on Azure but reaches other clouds only through AWS. What that means is that Microsoft's real protection with OpenAI has become mostly economic.

## What the word stateless keeps on Azure

The October 28 agreement already split OpenAI's products by cloud, since Microsoft's post on it said [API products OpenAI develops with third parties would be exclusive to Azure](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2025/10/28/the-next-chapter-of-the-microsoft-openai-partnership/) and non-API products could run on any cloud. The February 27 statement draws the line with a different word. It says Azure is the exclusive cloud for stateless APIs for OpenAI's models, whether customers buy them from Microsoft or from OpenAI, and that stateless calls coming out of OpenAI's collaborations with third parties, Amazon included, would be hosted on Azure. It also says OpenAI's first-party products, including its new Frontier agent platform, stay on Azure, and that the revenue share is unchanged and has always included OpenAI's revenue from partnerships with other clouds. The statement never defines stateless, and going by the public posts, the claim that nothing changed holds only if a product that keeps state always counted as something other than an API product. My read is that Microsoft kept the plain model calls and a share of revenue from OpenAI's partnerships with other clouds, and the word stateless shows where the Amazon deal fits.

## What Amazon is building on Bedrock

The new product shows up in Amazon's announcement, where Amazon says it is investing [$50 billion in OpenAI](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/aws/amazon-open-ai-strategic-partnership-investment), $15 billion up front and $35 billion later once certain conditions are met, and the two companies are jointly developing a Stateful Runtime Environment powered by OpenAI's models on Amazon Bedrock, Amazon's service for building with AI models, expected to launch in the next few months. AWS also becomes the exclusive third-party cloud distribution provider for Frontier, which OpenAI [launched on February 5 as a platform for enterprises to build and manage agents](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/05/openai-launches-a-way-for-enterprises-to-build-and-manage-ai-agents/). OpenAI's existing $38 billion agreement with AWS grows by $100 billion over eight years, with OpenAI using about 2 gigawatts of Trainium, Amazon's own AI chip.

Neither document says directly whether the model calls inside a stateful session count as stateless, though Amazon says the 2 gigawatts of Trainium will support demand for the Stateful Runtime Environment and Frontier, which reads to me like at least some of that work running on AWS, even as the joint statement says Frontier stays hosted on Azure. If those calls count as stateless calls, Azure serves them and Amazon holds the state around them, and if they don't, Azure's exclusivity only reaches calls that never pass through the runtime. Either way, for agents built on the Bedrock runtime, the context an agent keeps about a customer's work, its identity, and its connections to the customer's tools and data would sit with Amazon, and I'd argue those are what make an agent expensive to move. Every agent keeps state somewhere, since it works across many steps, so my verdict is that a line drawn around stateless calls leaves out the part of agent software that holds the state. In November I argued that [Microsoft's enterprise bet is the governed layer around models](/writing/microsoft-ignite-agent-365-governed-layer), meaning the identities, permissions, and registry every agent needs, and the runtime and Frontier together look like a version of that layer for OpenAI's models, with Amazon holding the runtime and the only third-party distribution of Frontier.

## Where Microsoft's protection actually sits

Microsoft is still well protected, and most of that protection is economic. The October agreement left Microsoft with about 27 percent of OpenAI on an as-converted diluted basis, a stake valued then at about $135 billion, along with OpenAI's contract to buy an incremental $250 billion of Azure services and IP rights to OpenAI's models and products through 2032, exclusive until an expert panel verifies AGI, and the February 27 statement said Microsoft's exclusive license continues unchanged. On January 28 Microsoft said [about 45 percent of its $625 billion in commercial remaining performance obligation](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/events/fy-2026/earnings-fy-2026-q2), meaning contracted business not yet booked as revenue at December 31, came from OpenAI. The rest of it is the set of other options Microsoft has, since the in-house frontier models Suleyman described are expected sometime in 2026, and on February 5 Microsoft put [Claude Opus 4.6 in Microsoft Foundry](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/claude-opus-4-6-anthropics-powerful-model-for-coding-agents-and-enterprise-workflows-is-now-available-in-microsoft-foundry-on-azure/), its service for building with AI models, the same day Anthropic released it.

Microsoft's defenders would point out that the revenue share includes revenue from OpenAI's partnerships with other clouds, that the runtime hasn't shipped, and that plain stateless calls are probably still most of OpenAI's API usage, though I haven't seen either company publish that split. I accept most of that, and I think it describes Microsoft as OpenAI's investor and supplier, which is a different position from being the platform where OpenAI's enterprise customers build. That position also concentrates risk, since about 45 percent of that backlog depends on one company, the kind of concentration I flagged in October when I wrote that [the circularity in these AI deals was the real worry](/writing/ai-circular-financing-2025). Where I come out is that Microsoft's hedge is its stake, its contracts, and the other models it can turn to, and that hedge is doing most of the work, since the cloud exclusivity stops at stateless calls.

If there is one thing I'd carry out of February's fine print, it is that a contract written around a technical term protects less once developers stop building the way the term describes. The October post drew the line at API products and the February statement at stateless calls, and both describe how developers used models when each was written. The share I'd watch through 2026 is how much of OpenAI's enterprise business moves into stateful products like the Bedrock runtime, because that decides how much the word stateless is worth to Azure, while under the terms as written, Microsoft collects through its stake and a revenue share that runs until AGI is verified and covers OpenAI's partnerships with other clouds.
