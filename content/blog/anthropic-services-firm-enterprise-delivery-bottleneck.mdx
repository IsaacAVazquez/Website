# Anthropic Is Building the Delivery Layer Because That Is Where Enterprise AI Is Stuck

On May 4, Anthropic and OpenAI each launched a company whose job is to get their models working inside other companies. [TechCrunch read the two ventures as one pattern](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/04/anthropic-and-openai-are-both-launching-joint-ventures-for-enterprise-ai-services/), in which each lab raises money from alternative asset managers to build new enterprise channels around Palantir-style forward-deployed engineers, and Fortune read Anthropic's as a shot at the consulting industry. Both readings are about who ends up with the money, and I care more about what problem each move solves for the customer. [Anthropic's announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/enterprise-ai-services-company) answered that with a line from CFO Krishna Rao, who said "Enterprise demand for Claude is significantly outpacing any single delivery model." In April I argued that [agentic AI was moving out of innovation budgets and into operating budgets](/writing/agentic-ai-line-item-not-pilot), a sign buyers had mostly stopped asking whether the models work. The next constraint is the delivery layer, meaning the people who can actually put the models to work. In May Anthropic built much of it, co-founding a services firm with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs that then bought Fractional AI, and shipping packaged agents for finance, legal, and small business itself. I think that is the right read of where enterprise AI is stuck, and the financial backers matter most as a channel into midsize companies the big integrators were unlikely to prioritize.

## The services firm and Fractional AI

The new firm will help midsize companies deploy Claude, and the examples Anthropic gave were community banks, manufacturers, and regional health systems. Anthropic's own applied AI engineers will work alongside the firm's engineers, and besides the three founding partners the firm is backed by General Atlantic, Leonard Green, Apollo, GIC, and Sequoia. TechCrunch, citing the Wall Street Journal, called the reported $1.5 billion a valuation, Fortune called it committed capital, and Anthropic's post disclosed no amounts. The venture's own May 21 statement, [as SiliconANGLE reported it](https://siliconangle.com/2026/05/21/new-anthropic-blackstone-backed-ai-services-firm-acquires-fractional-ai/), settled it as $1.5 billion committed, including $300 million each from Anthropic, Blackstone, and Hellman & Friedman and $150 million from Goldman Sachs.

I'd put more weight on the names, because the backers look to me like the plan for finding customers. Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, Apollo, and Leonard Green all own portfolios of operating companies, and I'd guess the first customers come from those portfolios, since a company is an easier sale when its owner also backs the firm doing the selling. As far as I can tell, the largest integrators organize their best people around the largest accounts, and a community bank or a regional manufacturer is the kind of customer I'd expect them to staff last.

The May 21 deal points the same way. The venture [bought Fractional AI](https://siliconangle.com/2026/05/21/new-anthropic-blackstone-backed-ai-services-firm-acquires-fractional-ai/), an applied AI services firm founded in 2024, on undisclosed terms. A services firm that young is mostly its people, and I read the purchase as a sign the venture wanted more delivery capacity than it could hire quickly, though buying a young firm's existing clients and playbook would look the same from the outside.

## The finance, legal, and small business packages

The product launches are the other half of the bet. Across eight days Anthropic released [ten ready-to-run agent templates for financial services](https://www.anthropic.com/news/finance-agents) on May 5, then the [legal package](https://claude.com/blog/claude-for-the-legal-industry) on May 12 with more than 20 connectors and 12 open-source practice-area plugins for Cowork, and then Claude for Small Business on May 13 with 15 ready-to-run workflows, 15 skills, and connections to tools like QuickBooks and HubSpot. The legal package names partners including Thomson Reuters' CoCounsel, Harvey, Legora, iManage, and Freshfields, and the small business launch came with an AI fluency course built with PayPal and a 10-city workshop tour, which is delivery work attached to a software launch.

A template is implementation work done once and shipped to everyone, so I read these packages as software doing the repeatable part of a services engagement, which leaves the engineers for the part that has to be done one customer at a time. I wrote requirements and acceptance criteria for the RunningMate launch at Civitech, and my view since then is that most of the work of deploying software for a customer is agreeing on what done means, which is the part no template can do.

On February 3, Thomson Reuters [fell nearly 18 percent, on track for its biggest one-day drop on record](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/anthropics-ai-tools-deepen-selloff-054551832.html), in a selloff Reuters tied to the Cowork plugins Anthropic had released on January 30. By May 12, CoCounsel is a named partner in Anthropic's legal package. I don't want to read too much into one partner list, but it at least shows the incumbent kept its seat inside the package after the selloff.

## PwC, KPMG, and the largest accounts

The same weeks showed where the integrators fit. On May 14 [PwC expanded its Claude partnership](https://www.anthropic.com/news/pwc-expanded-partnership), with plans to train and certify 30,000 US professionals on Claude. On May 19 KPMG gave all of its more than 276,000 employees access to Claude. Both follow Accenture, which formed an Anthropic Business Group in December, and at the time I read that deal as [a test of whether Anthropic's lead with developers could widen into a broad enterprise one](/writing/anthropic-enterprise-lead-narrower-than-40-percent). May looks like the next step, split by customer size, with the integrators taking the largest companies and the new firm going after the midsize ones below them.

The strongest case against this is that services are a poor business for a model company to own. They carry thin margins and grow with headcount, and a product that needed less implementation would not need so many engineers to sell it. OpenAI's same-day Deployment Company, which TechCrunch reported as raising $4 billion from 19 investors against a $10 billion valuation, could also mean the two labs are simply copying each other. I concede the margins point, and the copying point has two readings, copying or two labs looking at the same customers and seeing the same problem, which one day of announcements can't separate. I still think the distance between what these models can do and what a midsize company has deployed is wide enough that the help getting them deployed is what customers are really paying for in 2026, and the packages are how Anthropic makes each engagement smaller over time.

Once a model company builds its own delivery arm, it ends up selling against the partners it pays to sell for it, and channel conflict is the price Anthropic paid for reading the bottleneck correctly. On March 12 Anthropic [committed $100 million for 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-partner-network) to its partner network, and the May 4 announcement put the new firm in that network alongside Accenture, Deloitte, and PwC, so Anthropic is both the network's sponsor and a co-owner of one of its members, with its own engineers inside it. The first real test will be an account both sides want, meaning a company big enough for PwC or Accenture to pursue and small enough for the new firm to pitch. If Anthropic's engineers end up on the venture's side of that deal, the partners will have a reason to put less of their own effort behind Claude, and that effort is how Claude reaches the largest accounts.
