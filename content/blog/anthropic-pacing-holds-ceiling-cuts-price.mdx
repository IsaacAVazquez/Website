# Anthropic's Version of Pacing Holds the Ceiling Still and Keeps Cutting the Price

In an essay he published in September, which [TechCrunch covered on September 12](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/12/anthropic-ceo-outlines-plan-to-pace-the-frontier/), Dario Amodei wrote that "[we must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models](https://www.darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier)," and the argument that followed was mostly about whether to take him at his word. Sam Altman and Elon Musk endorsed it, and the skeptics TechCrunch [gathered on September 17](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/17/is-the-ai-safety-debate-about-safety-or-control/) read it as regulatory capture aimed at smaller companies or, in Cohere's Aidan Gomez's framing, a fight over who writes the guardrails. I can't check anyone's sincerity from the outside, but I can check what Anthropic's product line did in September, on either side of the essay. What I think that shows is that pacing, as Anthropic practices it, holds the top tier's name and price in place while the capability underneath gets cheaper, and moves the competition onto price and distribution.

The top of the lineup got its point release on September 1, when [Claude Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-1) shipped as one model with two levels of safeguards. Since it came before the essay, I read it as the practice the essay later put into words. The list price stayed at Fable 5's $10 and $50 per million input and output tokens. Anthropic estimated typical workloads would cost around 25 percent less than on Fable 5, and it said biology safeguards now fire 85 percent less often on benign biology questions. In June I argued that [the guarantees enterprise buyers need](/writing/anthropic-fable-5-three-guarantees-enterprise-buyers) matter more than Fable's capability lead, and Fable 5.1, together with the [Enterprise Frontier Safeguards](https://www.anthropic.com/news/enterprise-frontier-safeguards) program Anthropic announced the same day, speaks directly to one of the three guarantees I named then, which was handling data on the customer's terms. Misuse records will stay in the customer's own cloud under customer-managed keys, phased in from fall 2026, with zero data retention on Fable for eligible customers until then. Neither announcement addressed honest answers or availability. So the top tier kept its name and price, and the release was mostly about cost and fewer blocked requests, though its benchmark scores rose too.

Three weeks later the tier below caught up. Anthropic's [launch post for Claude Opus 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-opus-5-5) on September 22 calls it Anthropic's first release since the company called for pacing the frontier, and Opus 5.5 lists at $4 and $20 per million tokens against Opus 5's $5 and $25. Anthropic says it performs at Fable 5.1's level on most work and, by its estimate at default settings, costs 40 percent less to run than Opus 5 on typical workloads. On list price it sits 60 percent below Fable 5.1. In July I argued that Opus 5 was [the best model Claude Pro can afford to include](/writing/anthropic-fable-credits-opus-5-subscription-ceiling), with Fable sold by use above it on Pro, and by Anthropic's account Opus 5.5 closes most of the distance between those two tiers.

I don't want to overstate how still the ceiling was. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, an agentic coding test in both launch posts, Anthropic put Fable 5.1 at 55.8 percent and Opus 5.5 at 66.4 percent, so the cheaper model scored higher, and the same table put OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra at 57.9 percent, slightly above Fable 5.1. The bigger concession is on the capabilities the essay is about. Anthropic says in the same post that Opus 5.5 is comparable to Mythos 5.1 in biology and cybersecurity, which is why it ships with safeguards similar to Fable 5.1's and why most cybersecurity tasks get rerouted to Opus 4.8. What that means is that the capability the essay worries about moved down the price curve to the $4 and $20 tier within a month. The only things that held were the top tier's name and its price. I think that is the only version of pacing a buyer could see in September, but it is much narrower than the capability slowdown Amodei's essay describes.

Most of the other launches went into where people work and how companies pay, work that started before the essay but matters more if the top tier stops being where the contest happens. On September 16 Anthropic announced that chat and Cowork would merge into one Claude app that works out what each request needs, rolling out first to Pro and Max, and launched Claude Docs and Slides the same day. On September 23 it opened [Claude Marketplace](https://claude.com/blog/claude-marketplace), with more than 2,000 connectors and plugins and Claude-powered software from companies such as CrowdStrike and Harvey, which enterprise customers can buy with a portion of their committed Anthropic spend. That matters most to me, because it turns committed budget into money for the tools around Claude, the way the big cloud marketplaces work. Anthropic hasn't disclosed a revenue share, and the 2,000 figure is a count of listings.

The limit on my reading is that pacing governs what Anthropic releases, and by its own account the research behind those releases may be starting to accelerate. Anthropic's [August Risk Report](https://www.anthropic.com/aug-2026-risk-report) described Model 2, an internal model somewhat more capable than Mythos 5 that it had no plans to release as of mid-July, and said it was seeing early signs that AI may be speeding up its own research. On September 4 Anthropic said a model roughly comparable to the public Fable 5.1 worked largely alone for 11 days on what it calls the first end-to-end computer-checked proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, which shows how long a public-tier model can now run unaided more than anything held back. Amodei's essay commits Anthropic to embedded outside evaluators but not to a slower release schedule, so the public ceiling holds one release decision at a time. That makes September's pacing a release policy, with Model 2 as the one disclosed case of capability kept off the market.

The objection I weigh most heavily is about competition. Holding the top still while rivals keep shipping could hand the frontier to OpenAI. In Ramp's July data on more than 70,000 US businesses, nearly 44 percent paid for Anthropic and nearly 40 percent for OpenAI, but OpenAI's share was [growing faster in the third quarter through that point](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/20/openai-is-gaining-on-anthropic-with-business-users-new-data-indicates/), TechCrunch reported on August 20. That shows business buyers move quickly, but Ramp economist Ara Kharazian put Fable 5's weak adoption down to its price and data retention requirements, not a held ceiling.

The idea I'd hold onto from September is that the contest moved from the top of the lineup to the price of the tier below, the app people work in, and the budget they buy from, whether pacing moved it there or ordinary efficiency did, and I can't tell those apart from outside. What would show pacing had become a real constraint is a rival opening a clear, sustained gap over Fable 5.1, not the few points GPT-6 Astra has on one coding test, while Anthropic keeps a more capable internal model off the market anyway, the way it said in August that it had no current plans to release Model 2, or a published limit on releases that embedded evaluators can check against what ships. Until then, I'd judge the essay by whether the ceiling holds once holding it starts to cost Anthropic something.
