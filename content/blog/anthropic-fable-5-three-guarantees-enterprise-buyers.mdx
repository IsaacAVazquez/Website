# Fable 5's Rough Start Shows What Enterprise Buyers Need Anthropic to Guarantee

Claude Fable 5 launched on June 9, and Anthropic has had it switched off for every user since the evening of June 12. The coverage of its first nine days has read them as three separate stories, from the argument over hidden safeguards, to the fight over data retention, to an export order read as a standoff between a government and a lab. I read them as one stress test of three things no benchmark measures, which are whether the model answers honestly, handles data on the customer's terms, and stays available. Anthropic committed to fixing the first the next day, but it imposed the second as a term buyers had to take or leave and lost the third to a government order. I think those guarantees now matter more to enterprise buyers than the capability gap that made Fable worth buying.

Anthropic's [launch post](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-fable-5-mythos-5) calls Fable 5 its most capable generally available model and prices it at $10 and $50 per million input and output tokens, twice Opus 4.8's price per token, alongside Mythos 5, a version with some safeguards lifted for Project Glasswing partners. Fable's safety classifiers for cybersecurity, biology and chemistry, and distillation attempts send a flagged request to Opus 4.8, and Anthropic said more than 95 percent of Fable sessions involve no fallback. That is a share of all Fable sessions, so up to about one session in twenty may hit at least one fallback, and a buyer whose work leans on security or biology could see far more and would see each one.

## The hidden safeguards on AI research

The hidden safeguards are the case Anthropic handled best. The system card, as [Willison quoted it on June 10](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jun/10/if-claude-fable-stops-helping-you/), said Anthropic would also limit Fable's effectiveness on frontier LLM development, meaning pretraining pipelines, distributed training infrastructure, and machine learning accelerator design, and that, unlike the classifiers, these would be invisible to the user. Anthropic estimated they would touch about 0.03 percent of traffic, concentrated in fewer than 0.1 percent of organizations. Willison objected to a model that gives some users worse answers without telling them, and by the night of June 10 Anthropic had [reversed course](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jun/11/anthropic-walks-back-policy/). It said it had "made the wrong tradeoff," apologized, and said flagged requests would start visibly falling back to Opus 4.8 that week, with the API returning a reason for each refusal within days, while the safeguards themselves stayed in place. The model was pulled two days later, so I can't say how much of that actually shipped.

The original design had a real case, since the slice was tiny and a visible safeguard shows anyone trying to evade it where the line sits. But the concentration cuts the other way, because the customers inside that slice would have gotten weaker answers with no sign anything had changed. In January I argued that a feature with a model in it [fails plausibly and quietly](/writing/on-call-for-ai-features), and the hidden safeguards would have produced that failure on purpose. Since May 27 I've been a growth intern at Juno, where part of my work is independently re-deriving published numbers, which has made me care about whether a tool shows me its failures. A visible fallback with a stated reason is the pattern I'd want for every safety intervention.

## The 30-day retention term

Retention is a defensible decision that Anthropic shipped in the wrong form. The launch post requires 30-day retention across all Fable 5 and Mythos 5 traffic so Anthropic can run its safety classifiers, with no zero data retention option, deleting the data after 30 days but holding content flagged for review longer. InfoQ reported on June 15 that Microsoft had [pulled Fable 5 from its internal Copilot model picker](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/06/claude-5-release/) because the retention term conflicts with its own zero-retention standard. GitHub, which Microsoft owns, kept offering it until the June 12 order on Copilot's Pro+, Max, Business, and Enterprise plans, switched off by default for business and enterprise accounts.

A model with Fable's cyber capability plausibly needs that monitoring, and since the data isn't used for training, I'd grant the term is the safety system working as designed. What I'd push back on is that buyers got no choice. A company whose own policies require zero retention can't accept the term, and Microsoft's response shows what that looks like, with GitHub passing the same decision to every business and enterprise customer's admin. A control that reasonable buyers land on differently should be something they can configure, and shipping it as take or leave meant a major partner's own employees lost access while it made up its mind.

## The June 12 export order

Anthropic had the least control over the export order. At 5:21 pm Eastern on June 12, the company [received a US export-control directive](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access) to cut off Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for any foreign national, inside or outside the United States. With no reliable way to verify nationality in real time, it disabled both models for every user while its other models kept running. Anthropic argued that the one narrow, possible jailbreak the government believed it had found was no reason to recall a commercial model available to hundreds of millions of people. [Fortune reported](https://fortune.com/2026/06/14/how-a-warning-from-amazon-led-the-white-house-to-shut-down-anthropics-mythos-model/) on June 14 that Amazon researchers had gotten Fable 5 to give out restricted cyberattack information, that Andy Jassy raised it with administration officials on June 11, and that Commerce gave Anthropic about 90 minutes to comply. Katie Moussouris of Luta Security [argued on June 14](https://www.lutasecurity.com/post/the-fable-5-export-controls-harm-us-cyber-defense) that the behavior was "the most valuable thing an AI model can do for defensive security."

I'd grant the government the authority to pull a model with Fable's cyber capability, and I can't judge from the public reporting whether this finding justified it. What the order means for buyers is that, since June 12, availability is partly a regulatory question, which I doubt any uptime commitment was written to cover. Both models are still off as I write this on June 18, so the practical answer is a tested fallback model that every workflow can run on if one vanishes overnight.

Each of the three decisions had a real argument behind it and still reached buyers without a choice, a warning, or both. In March I argued that the narrowness of Anthropic's red lines on military use [was part of what let them hold up](/writing/anthropic-pentagon-red-lines-usage-policy-product), because a policy written like a spec let partners tell quickly whether it applied to them. I think a buyer's contract with a frontier lab should be written the same way, from a clause that every safety intervention shows up as a refusal or a stated fallback, to retention terms the buyer can negotiate, to a named fallback model at a known price and a promise about how quickly the customer hears of a suspension. A buyer could fairly answer that capability still decides these purchases while nothing else matches Fable, and I'd grant that as of June 18, but a buyer can weigh a capability gap before signing and can only weigh these three risks if the contract names them. Anthropic confidentially submitted a [draft S-1](https://www.anthropic.com/news/confidential-draft-s1-sec) on June 1, and I'd expect all three risks among its risk factors if it becomes public. I'd rather buyers see them in their contracts first.
