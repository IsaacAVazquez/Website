# Apple Gave Ternus the Products and Kept Tim Cook for the Policymakers

The new CEO's name was the least surprising part of Apple's April 20 announcement. The Financial Times reported in November 2025 that Apple had intensified preparations for Tim Cook to step down as soon as 2026, with John Ternus the likeliest successor, and most of the coverage I read treated the news as continuity. Fortune quoted Bloomberg Intelligence's Anurag Rana saying the pick signaled ["continuity rather than strategic change,"](https://fortune.com/2026/04/21/apple-stock-slipping-tim-cook-john-ternus-wall-street-says-buy/) and Ben Thompson's [Stratechery essay](https://stratechery.com/2026/tim-cooks-impeccable-timing/) credited Cook's timing while faulting App Store pricing, Apple's dependence on China, and the decision to white-label Google's Gemini for Siri.

I think the most consequential line in [Apple's release](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/04/tim-cook-to-become-apple-executive-chairman-john-ternus-to-become-apple-ceo/) is about the job Cook kept. On September 1 Ternus, who joined Apple in 2001 and rose to lead its hardware engineering, becomes CEO and joins the board, and Cook, who stays CEO through the summer, becomes executive chairman. The release says Cook "will assist with certain aspects of the company, including engaging with policymakers around the world," and policymakers are the only part of the job it names. What's interesting is that App Store pricing and the dependence on China, two of the things Thompson faulted, are problems Apple works out partly with regulators and trade officials, which is the work the release asks Cook to help with. I read that as Apple dividing its top job in two, with an engineer running the products and its most experienced negotiator helping with policymakers, and I'd argue that's the right design for a year shaped by tariffs, US manufacturing pledges, and court fights over App Store fees, as long as the line between the two jobs stays clear.

## The load Cook is carrying into the chair

Apple's policy and legal calendar since February shows what that job is up against. On February 20 the Supreme Court ruled 6 to 3 in Learning Resources v. Trump that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, the law behind the reciprocal tariffs, doesn't authorize tariffs, and a temporary global tariff under a different law took effect on February 24. By CNBC's tally Apple had paid about $3.3 billion in tariffs of all kinds from the start of the 2025 tariffs through the December 2025 quarter, not all of it necessarily refundable, and CNBC framed the refund question as a choice between seeking the money and absorbing the cost to avoid antagonizing the White House, which makes it a relationship question as much as a legal one. Cook said on Good Morning America on March 17 that Apple is "monitoring the situation" and will "decide accordingly" once the courts rule. On February 24, four days after the ruling, Apple said it will [start building Mac mini in Houston](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/02/apple-accelerates-us-manufacturing-with-mac-mini-production/) later in 2026 and buy well over 100 million advanced chips from TSMC's Arizona plant in 2026, which Apple presented as progress on the $600 billion, four-year US commitment Cook announced at the White House alongside President Trump in August 2025.

Then there's Epic, where on April 3 Apple asked the Ninth Circuit to [pause the proceedings](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/04/06/apple-epic-games-supreme-court-petition/) that will decide what commission, if any, it can charge on purchases made through outside links while it petitions the Supreme Court. The court granted the pause on April 6, and Epic, which called the request another delay tactic to protect "junk fees," asked it to reconsider. Keeping up with all of that is close to a full-time job, and on the tariff and manufacturing side especially, the relationships have run through Cook.

## What the split gives Ternus

The product job Ternus inherits is already full. Apple named Johny Srouji chief hardware officer the same day, effective immediately, so the group Ternus ran has a new owner before he takes the top job. The harder problem is people, and on April 21 Bloomberg framed [keeping Apple's top talent](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-21/apple-ceo-ternus-confronts-test-of-retaining-company-s-top-talent) as Ternus's test and reported that Mike Rockwell, who has run the Siri overhaul since March 2025, has considered leaving or moving into an advisory role in 2027, though he's unlikely to go before the Siri upgrade is done. That upgrade is the more personalized Siri Apple has promised for 2026, and it wasn't in iOS 26.4 when that shipped on March 24. The work in front of Ternus runs from keeping the people who build the next Siri, to overseeing a reorganized hardware group, to shipping what Apple has promised, and I think having Cook help carry the policy work, tariffs above all, gives that agenda room it wouldn't otherwise have.

## Where the two jobs overlap

The worry I take most seriously is that executive chairmen who stay close to the business can turn into shadow CEOs, and a new CEO whose predecessor keeps the most sensitive outside relationships can struggle to establish authority with regulators, partners, and his own team. The relationships also belong to Apple, and there's a fair argument they should move with the title, especially since Cook's standing with the Trump administration helps only as long as that administration is the one Apple has to deal with. Cook told employees on April 21 that he plans to stay in the new role a long time, so the arrangement is meant to last. And the two jobs overlap more than an org chart suggests, because policy fights keep turning into product decisions. On March 12 Apple said it would cut its standard App Store commission in China from 30 percent to 25 percent, [following discussions with the Chinese regulator](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=dadukodv). When the FTC chair warned Apple in February about how Apple News curates stories, the [letter was addressed to Apple CEO Tim Cook](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/02/federal-trade-commission-chairman-andrew-n-ferguson-issues-warning-letter-apple-ceo-tim-cook), and after September 1 a letter like that would presumably go to Ternus while Cook is the one helping handle policymakers. In October 2025 I argued that [the value of Apple's privacy promise is in being able to predict when Apple will stand up for its users](/writing/apple-privacy-line-moves-with-who-is-asking), and that Apple's privacy line held against attacks on the phone's code but moved depending on which government was asking, so I think splitting who draws it between two people makes it harder to predict.

I grant all of that, and I still think the split is right, because Apple's exposure in 2026 is unusual. A company with an open refund question, a $600 billion pledge to deliver, and App Store terms being set by courts and regulators needs a senior person spending real time on policymakers, and a new CEO trying to ship the next Siri shouldn't have to learn those relationships in his first months.

What this succession says to me is that once a company's hardest product decisions start getting set in courtrooms and trade offices, the CEO job holds two jobs, and being clear about who does which matters more than finding one person who can do both. The first test is who fronts Apple's next tariff or App Store fight once the handoff takes effect on September 1. If Ternus is the one explaining what changes for customers and developers while Cook deals with the officials behind it, the split is doing its job. If Cook ends up explaining both, Apple will have a new CEO without a change in who speaks for the company.
