# GitHub Cut Copilot's Plans a Week Before It Explained the Meter

Between April 10 and April 22, GitHub paused Copilot Pro trials and new sign-ups for several plans, tightened usage limits, and pulled Anthropic's Opus models out of its $10 Pro plan. On April 27 GitHub explained that every Copilot plan would [move to usage-based billing](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/github-copilot-is-moving-to-usage-based-billing/) on June 1. A developer in GitHub's own community thread [called the April 20 changes a bait-and-switch](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/192963#discussioncomment-16640945) the day they landed, and on Microsoft's April 29 earnings call the same product came up as growth, with Satya Nadella saying nearly 140,000 organizations use GitHub Copilot. I think both readings miss the order things happened in. GitHub was right to meter Copilot, because coding agents broke the old pricing, but a meter only works if the people paying it believe it is fair, and cutting models and sign-ups on April 20, a week before explaining the new model, made a fair design look like a price increase in disguise.

The economics are the part GitHub got right. Copilot's paid plans have been priced around what GitHub calls premium requests, which count requests weighted by a per-model multiplier, so a request cost the same however much work the model did on it, while what model providers actually charge for is tokens, the chunks of text a model reads and writes, and an agent working through a codebase can use far more of them than a chat reply. GitHub's April 27 post said that under premium requests a user can pay the same for a quick chat question as for hours of autonomous coding, and it called that model "no longer sustainable." On that call Amy Hood, Microsoft's chief financial officer, forecast a Microsoft Cloud gross margin of about 64 percent for the June quarter and [named increased GitHub Copilot usage](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/events/fy-2026/earnings-fy-2026-q3) as one of the things weighing on it. When requests that cost very different amounts carry one price, light users pay for heavy ones until the company caps the heavy users or charges them. I think metering by the token is the honest way to price agent work. In April GitHub did both, capping first and announcing the charge second.

That order is where I think GitHub made its mistake. On April 10 it paused new Pro trials, citing abuse of its trial system, and [announced new limits](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-04-10-enforcing-new-limits-and-retiring-opus-4-6-fast-from-copilot-pro) to roll out over the following weeks after a rise in heavy use. On April 20 it [paused sign-ups for Pro, Pro+, and Student](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-04-20-changes-to-github-copilot-plans-for-individuals), tightened usage limits for individual plans, removed Opus models from Pro while keeping Opus 4.7 on the $39 Pro+ plan, and offered prorated refunds to those who canceled by May 20. On April 22 it [paused self-serve Copilot Business sign-ups](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-04-22-pausing-new-self-serve-signups-for-github-copilot-business) for organizations on GitHub's Free and Team plans. GitHub's April 20 blog post did say a handful of requests could now cost more than the plan price, and it promised "a more sustainable solution" without naming it or giving a date, so customers had a promise and no price. From April 20, and in smaller ways since April 10, customers could see what they were losing without seeing the pricing that would replace it, which made a price increase the easiest explanation to believe. One reply in the billing post's thread [said losing the models that used to be free destroyed the value of the individual plans](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/192948#discussioncomment-16731489). In January I argued that [Anthropic drew the right line on subscriptions and enforced it the wrong way](/writing/anthropic-subscription-harness-block-developer-trust), and the lesson about announcing before enforcing carries over.

A meter asks for more trust than a flat price does. With a flat price, a customer knows the bill in advance and only has to decide whether the product is worth it. With a meter, the customer is trusting that the count is accurate and the rates are fair, for work they mostly can't see. I write implementation code for projects on this site with Claude, and from my side of the screen a quick question and a request to build a feature both look like one prompt, though the model does far more work for the second. What that means is that a developer on a metered plan can't easily know what a request will cost before sending it, so their sense of whether the meter is fair comes mostly from how GitHub has treated them, and what GitHub showed them first was a product getting smaller. On Microsoft's call, RBC analyst Rishi Jaluria raised the enterprise version of the point, asking how Microsoft balances the predictability of paying per seat with growing consumption.

GitHub can fairly call the rationing triage, and I take that seriously. On April 28 GitHub's chief technology officer wrote that agent-driven development had [picked up sharply since December 2025](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/an-update-on-github-availability/), that a plan begun in October 2025 to grow capacity tenfold had given way in February 2026 to designing for what he called 30 times today's scale. GitHub's April 10 changelog had already said heavy use "places significant strain on our shared infrastructure," and Hood said Azure would stay capacity constrained at least through 2026. Pausing sign-ups was a reasonable way to protect existing customers.

The design deserves credit too, even though heavy agent users will probably pay more under it, which is the point of a meter. Every paid plan keeps its price and includes the same dollar amount in monthly credits, $10 for Pro, $39 for Pro+, $19 per user for Business, and $39 per user for Enterprise, and existing Business and Enterprise customers get promotional allotments of $30 and $70 in monthly credits for June through August. Credits are drawn down at each model's published API rates, which anyone can check. Inline code completions stay unmetered, admins can cap spending, and customers got five weeks of notice. The design is a price increase for some groups, though, since model multipliers go up on June 1 for annual Pro and Pro+ subscribers and the fallback to a cheaper model when usage runs out goes away for everyone. What I don't accept is that triage required customers to take the April 20 cuts a week before seeing the pricing behind them. The April 27 post itself said the April changes were made "as we prepare for the broader transition to usage-based billing" and that GitHub "will loosen usage limits" once it takes effect, so the cuts were a bridge, and one dated sentence on April 20 saying so would have let customers read them that way. The design is sound, but the order cost goodwill it didn't need to spend.

What I keep coming back to is that customers judge a meter on whether it seems fair before they judge the price, and the first rules a company announces decide how every later bill gets read. Nadella told analysts on April 29 that every Microsoft business priced per user, from productivity to coding to security, will come to charge for usage as well, which makes GitHub's rollout an early look at how Microsoft introduces a meter. I've argued that [pricing is product work](/writing/pricing-strategy-initiative), because each pricing change a company can't defend cleanly makes the next one harder to explain to customers. I think GitHub can defend most of its April 27 design, but it arrived after the week from April 20 in which developers had only the cuts to go on, and I'd expect the first metered bills after June 1 to be read against that week before anyone checks the rates.
