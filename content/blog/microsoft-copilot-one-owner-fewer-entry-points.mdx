# Microsoft Started Fixing Copilot in March by Taking Things Away

The two Copilot decisions I'd defend hardest from Microsoft's March both took something away. On March 17 Satya Nadella gave the Copilot experience a single owner across consumer and commercial, and on March 20 the head of Windows promised to cut what he called unnecessary Copilot entry points, starting with Snipping Tool, Photos, Widgets and Notepad. Much of the coverage I read treated March as retreats and exits. The Verge's Tom Warren wrote that Microsoft had needed this moment for years, but he also found it hard not to read the reorganization as [an admission](https://www.theverge.com/news/895963/microsoft-copilot-leadership-changes-consumer-commercial) that splitting Copilot for consumers and businesses had failed, TechCrunch headlined the Windows news as Microsoft [rolling back some of its Copilot AI bloat](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/20/microsoft-rolls-back-some-of-its-copilot-ai-bloat-on-windows/), and GeekWire had framed Rajesh Jha's retirement the week before as [the latest exit](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/microsoft-evp-rajesh-jha-retiring-after-35-years-in-latest-exit-from-senior-leadership-team/) from Microsoft's senior leadership. I read the same three moves as the start of a Copilot plan I can actually follow, with Jha's exit as the setup and the March 17 and March 20 decisions as the plan. Both decisions treat accountability and user trust as the scarce inputs, and I think a company that has spent years adding Copilot to more of its software needed both before asking people to let agents act for them.

Jha's retirement on March 12 set this up. He ran Experiences + Devices, the group behind Microsoft 365 Copilot, Office, Teams and Windows, and he said he'll [transition out on July 1](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/03/12/microsoft-announces-experiences-devices-leadership-changes/). Microsoft named no single successor, and Jha's memo named four executives who will report directly to Nadella as executive vice presidents. Per The Verge, Perry Clarke runs the core of Microsoft 365, Charles Lamanna business and industry Copilot, Pavan Davuluri Windows and Devices, and Ryan Roslansky, LinkedIn's CEO, Office. I think this will take a management layer out from between Copilot's pieces and the CEO. The risk is that four executives who each own a place where Copilot shows up can just as easily become four Copilot owners, which is why the following week mattered more.

The March 17 memo is the Copilot decision I'd rank above everything else Microsoft did in March, including the March 9 research preview of Copilot Cowork. Nadella made Jacob Andreou, a former Snap executive who had been Microsoft AI's corporate vice president of product and growth, [executive vice president of Copilot](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/03/17/announcing-copilot-leadership-update/), leading the Copilot experience across consumer and commercial and "driving design, product, growth, and engineering." Mustafa Suleyman, who had run consumer Copilot as head of Microsoft AI, will focus on superintelligence and frontier models, and both sit on a Copilot Leadership Team with the executives who run the Copilot platform and the Microsoft 365 apps. Nadella described the goal as moving "from a collection of great products to a truly integrated system." I think one accountable owner beats several teams that can each ship a Copilot button, because when a feature spans products that belong to different groups, no one person answers for whether the whole thing works. Warren's story said the change should partly help address "the fact that nobody really owns Copilot inside Microsoft," and I'd call that the more important half of his read.

Davuluri's March 20 post applies the same idea to Windows itself. He wrote that Microsoft will be ["more intentional about how and where Copilot integrates across Windows,"](https://blogs.windows.com/windows-insider/2026/03/20/our-commitment-to-windows-quality/) starting by "reducing unnecessary Copilot entry points" in apps like those four, and he listed that next to a movable taskbar, longer update pauses and a faster File Explorer. I think putting Copilot on the same list as update fixes is the most honest thing in the post, and it fits his line that "reliability is the bedrock of trust." An entry point measures exposure, meaning how many people saw a way into Copilot, and it says very little about whether anyone wanted to go in. In October I argued that Microsoft could [bundle Copilot into Microsoft 365 as long as the exit stays visible](/writing/microsoft-consumer-copilot-bundle-visible-exit), and a Copilot button nobody asked for has the same problem as a Microsoft 365 plan without Copilot that subscribers struggle to find, since the usage it produces fits two stories, one where people wanted Copilot and one where they couldn't avoid it.

Entry points are also how an assistant gets discovered and becomes a habit, and by the measures public in March, both paid and consumer Copilot had little adoption to spare. GeekWire, by its own math, put paid Microsoft 365 Copilot at [about 3 percent](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/microsoft-revamps-copilot-structure-elevating-former-snap-exec-as-suleyman-shifts-to-ai-models/) of the commercial base, dividing the 15 million paid seats Microsoft reported for the December quarter by more than 450 million commercial seats, and cited Statcounter estimates that put Copilot's share of chatbot web traffic in the low single digits. In January Microsoft also said consumer Copilot app daily users were up nearly 3x over a year, with no base given. The Windows buttons Davuluri is cutting feed that consumer product, and none of these numbers counts them, but the two shares point the same way. Pulling entry points could shrink that share while people build habits elsewhere. The other objections are fair too, starting with Jha's memo, which says the details will be settled by June, so a skeptic could file March 12 under routine housekeeping. The Verge wrote that Davuluri's list had [no direct commitment to respect browser defaults](https://www.theverge.com/news/897834/microsoft-windows-11-quality-performance-commitments-changes), and the post promises Insider previews in March and April with no date for everyone else. Consumer and enterprise assistants may also need different products, which one owner could blur.

I take the discovery argument seriously, and I think the low share cuts both ways. It could mean Copilot needs every entry point it can get, or it could mean years of entry points weren't turning into habits, and neither share figure nor the 3x growth settles which, since none of these numbers sees how people use Copilot inside Windows. What tips me is what Microsoft wants people to do next. Microsoft described Copilot Cowork, which it built with Anthropic, as meant for "long-running, multi-step work that unfolds over time," which means trusting software to take steps on someone's behalf. I'd guess people grant that to products they trust and to companies where someone is plainly responsible, and a Copilot button nobody asked for costs some of that trust, while a Copilot split across several groups made the responsible person hard to find. In January I argued that Microsoft should be judged on [whether Copilot usage earns first claim on its scarce GPUs](/writing/microsoft-gpu-allocation-copilot-over-azure), and usage from people who went looking for Copilot is the kind that would earn it.

March convinced me that putting a product in more places only helps once someone answers for it. Adding an entry point is probably one of the cheapest things a product team can do, so when nobody answers for the whole, entry points keep multiplying whether or not people use them. Since March 17, Andreou has answered for the Copilot experience across consumer and commercial, and Davuluri has committed in writing to fewer Copilot entry points, though I don't know yet whether either change will stick. The standard I'd hold Microsoft to is that the next time Copilot shows up somewhere new, a specific person can say why it belongs there and what it did for the people who used it. For an assistant that wants permission to act for people, one owner and fewer entry points come first, and in March Microsoft started on both.
