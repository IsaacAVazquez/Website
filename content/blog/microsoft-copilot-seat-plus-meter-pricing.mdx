# Microsoft's New Copilot Turns the Seat Into an Entry Fee and Makes the Meter the Product

Microsoft's rebuilt Copilot, introduced on September 25, comes with two prices. The Register's Carly Page [read the launch as a super app with a meter attached](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/09/28/microsofts-copilot-super-app-comes-with-a-meter-attached/5299515), while GeekWire's Todd Bishop [framed it as a push for adoption against Anthropic and OpenAI](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/microsoft-unveils-all-in-one-copilot-app-taking-on-anthropic-and-openai-in-new-push-to-boost-adoption/) and quoted Jared Spataro, Microsoft's chief marketing officer for AI at Work, describing two kinds of AI at work that call for two ways of paying. I think Spataro's logic holds, because it makes the seat an entry fee and the meter the product. It's the case for metering agent work I made about GitHub in April, but here it moves the forecasting risk onto customers with little history to forecast from, and it only works if Microsoft's spend controls earn a kind of trust its billing hasn't shown yet.

The app Spataro [introduced on September 25](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/09/25/introducing-the-new-copilot-with-home-code-and-autopilot/) has three parts. Home merges Copilot Chat and Copilot Cowork, Code lets people who don't write software build apps and automations, and Autopilot is a persistent agent for recurring work. The seat covers Copilot Chat, Copilot in Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook and Teams, and an Auto router that picks a model for each request. The meter covers Copilot Cowork, Code, Autopilot, other long-running agent work, and frontier models, among them the two the post calls Astra and Fable, which GeekWire identified as OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra and Anthropic's Claude Fable. The post gave no price for the seat and no rates for the meter.

I think the line sits in the right place. In a [September 1 post](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/09/01/the-yield-imperative-turning-ai-infrastructure-into-useful-intelligence/), Microsoft wrote that one agentic task can use more than 3,400 times the tokens of a typical chat interaction, and while the post cites no source or method for that figure, it's the company's own description of how far apart the two ends of the workload can get in tokens, before differences in each model's price per token stretch or shrink that gap. A flat seat can absorb the chat end of that range but not the agent end, and a meter lets the price move with what the work costs to run. Code and Autopilot sit on the meter and Home is split across the line, which is why I'd call the meter the product, and why I'd guess how clearly Home shows which side a request lands on will matter about as much as the rates.

The seat and meter structure has been spreading through Microsoft all year. On April 29 Satya Nadella told analysts that every business priced per user would end up charging per user and for usage, and the next day I argued that [GitHub was right to meter Copilot and wrong to ration it before explaining the meter](/writing/github-copilot-usage-billing-order-of-operations). GitHub moved every Copilot plan to token-metered AI Credits on June 1, and when Copilot Cowork reached [general availability on June 16](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/blog/2026/06/16/copilot-cowork-is-now-generally-available/), it required the Microsoft 365 Copilot license and billed usage on top of it in Copilot Credits, at a cent each on pay-as-you-go. In September GitHub changed both halves. On September 3 it [began reopening Copilot Business and Enterprise sign-ups](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-09-03-reopening-copilot-business-and-enterprise-signups) for customers who pay by credit card or PayPal, on the condition that every new seat is paid for before the user gets access, and from October 1 those card and PayPal customers, existing ones included, will be charged upfront at the start of each cycle. On September 16 it made [budget increase requests generally available](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-09-16-copilot-budget-increase-requests-are-generally-available), so a Business or Enterprise member who runs out of AI credits can ask an admin for more, and gets access back if the admin approves.

The company has converged on a seat plus a meter. GitHub's budget requests turn running out of credits into a decision an admin makes, and the controls for tracking and capping AI spend that Microsoft announced on September 25 under the FinOps label bring the same idea to Copilot, letting admins route employees' credit requests into approval workflows and extending to Code the cost management that already covered Copilot Cowork. Admins have been able to cap Copilot Cowork spending since June 16, but with Code, Autopilot and the frontier models still unpriced, and Copilot Cowork priced per credit with no published count of credits per task, no buyer can model the bill yet.

The spread in tokens between a chat and an agent task is also the risk, and a meter hands it to the buyer. I wrote in May about [why agent bills are hard to forecast](/writing/what-an-ai-agent-actually-costs-in-production), since a small share of long runs drives most of the spend. In late May TechCrunch reported developers [posting claims and a screenshot](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/30/what-a-joke-github-copilots-new-token-based-billing-spurs-consternation-among-devs/), none of them verified, that projected their GitHub Copilot costs rising from about $29 a month to nearly $750, or from about $50 a month to about $3,000, while defenders blamed inefficient use. Both readings can be true, and either way those developers found out after the price was set. The Register quoted Simon Baxter of TechMarketView saying customers who want the agent features need a consumption mindset and the cost governance that goes with it, because an agent working without prompts is no longer tied to someone at a keyboard, which describes Autopilot.

Microsoft would reply that large enterprises already buy Azure this way, with FinOps teams and commitment discounts, and that the everyday tier stays flat. I think that's right, and it's why the model can work. Where it's weaker is who starts the spending. Azure consumption mostly follows systems that engineers design and size, while the Copilot meter can start whenever someone in finance or sales hands a task to an agent. During [my growth internship at Juno](/writing/juno-mba-growth-internship) I used multi-agent AI workflows to re-derive published numbers independently, and under this pricing that kind of work would sit on the meter. A flat seat is also easy for a CFO to approve, since the bill is known in advance. GeekWire put the more than 30 million paid Microsoft 365 Copilot seats Microsoft reported in July at about 7 percent of the more than 450 million commercial seats Microsoft cited in January. GeekWire worked that ratio out itself, and since both figures are "more than" counts, 7 percent is an approximation that could land a little on either side. At a paid share that small, the features this relaunch is built around sit in the part of the price that's hardest to approve. A metered buyer needs to see spending before agents run on their own, and the controls Microsoft announced are necessary and still unproven.

Across Microsoft's year of AI pricing, every price it set was a decision about who holds the risk. GitHub's credits and Copilot Cowork's credits handed the variable cost of agent work to customers, and the September 25 split draws that line inside one app, where everyday use stays on a flat seat and Microsoft holds the risk of that use. I think the risk of agent work belongs with the customer, who decides how much of it to buy, but putting it on the customer's budget line moves the burden of proof onto Microsoft's billing. When Microsoft publishes rates, the first thing I'd check is whether a buyer can estimate a month of Autopilot before turning it on, since I'd guess most IT departments won't let an agent run for a quarter until they trust both the count and the budget cap.
