# The Xbox Reset Reads as a Verdict on Buying Small Studios for Game Pass

At E3 in June 2018, Microsoft announced it was buying Ninja Theory, Undead Labs and Compulsion Games, along with Playground Games, deals [TechCrunch tied to Microsoft's push to strengthen Xbox Game Pass](https://techcrunch.com/2018/06/10/microsoft-acquires-a-whole-bunch-of-game-studios), and a year later it announced it was buying Double Fine. On July 6 Xbox said Ninja Theory, Undead Labs, Compulsion and Double Fine were all leaving, the same day Microsoft said it was eliminating [about 4,800 roles, about 2.1% of its global workforce](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/07/06/the-latest-in-our-company-transformation/). TechCrunch [covered the day as a layoff story](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/06/microsoft-lays-off-nearly-5000-employees-across-xbox-commercial-sales/). That frame is fair, and it fits what I wrote in April about [a tech job market splitting in two](/writing/tech-job-market-splitting-2026-macro-backdrop). What I'd add is that Asha Sharma's memo that day, ["Resetting XBOX,"](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2026/07/06/resetting-xbox/) reads as a strategy document about Game Pass. I think her figures and what Xbox did over the rest of July show that owning more and more small studios was the wrong way to build a subscription platform, and that Microsoft is trying to grow Game Pass through the players it can reach, even though that thins the catalog people subscribe for.

Sharma, who took over from Phil Spencer as CEO of Microsoft Gaming on February 20, is shrinking Xbox by about 3,200 people across the fiscal year that began July 1, and her reasons are what I'd defend first. She wrote that "Our business today is not healthy," said Xbox runs "at margins that are 3-10x lower than comparable platform and publishing businesses," and added that "we are not the best home for every type of studio; in a typical year, we lost 64 cents for every dollar we invested." I read that figure as describing only the studios Xbox isn't the best home for, not the whole business. Hardware weighs on the margins too, since Xbox cited higher storage and memory costs when it said on June 25 that console prices would rise on August 1. But the business she calls unhealthy is also the one Microsoft spent five years adding studios to, from E3 2018 to closing its Activision Blizzard purchase in October 2023, and her memo says as much. "Since 2018, we have aggressively expanded our studio portfolio," she wrote, and the bets on Game Pass, multiplatform and more content "did not grow at the pace we expected." She doesn't put the whole gap on studios, though. She wrote that Xbox "entered Gen 9 with a smaller install base and a higher cost structure" and that its platform teams are "40% larger than they were at the start of this generation," and she had Mojang and King, both acquired, report directly to her, so Xbox is keeping its biggest acquisitions close while letting smaller studios go.

The portfolio changes follow from that. Compulsion Games and Double Fine become independent studios that keep the rights to their games, and Ninja Theory and Undead Labs "have entered terms to join new ownership" with funding to finish their current games. Helen Chiang moves into a new chief operating officer role with profit and loss responsibility, and Sharma wrote that "None of our first party publicly announced games or projects are being cancelled as part of these reductions." I think these are the right cuts for a publisher that wants fewer, clearer franchises, and they are gentler than closures. One executive owning the whole profit and loss is the same bet on accountability I liked in March, when Microsoft [gave Copilot a single owner across consumer and commercial](/writing/microsoft-copilot-one-owner-fewer-entry-points).

The rest of July pushed further on a Game Pass strategy that started before the reset, since Xbox had already put a starter Game Pass inside Discord Nitro on May 11. On July 21 eligible subscribers to Meta Horizon+ began getting [a starter edition of Game Pass](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2026/07/21/xbox-game-pass-meta-horizon/) with more than 50 games and 10 hours of cloud gaming a month. On July 22 Xbox brought backward compatibility to Windows PCs and handhelds such as the ROG Ally, and on July 23 it began a test for Xbox Insiders that lets players [stream select games they own for free](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2026/07/23/game-streaming-ad-supported-xbox-insiders/), with ads before sessions and one-hour session limits. That builds on April 21, when Xbox [cut Game Pass Ultimate from $29.99 to $22.99 a month](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2026/04/21/xbox-game-pass-update/) in the US and moved new Call of Duty games, a franchise that came with Activision Blizzard, from launch day to about a year after launch. That last change tells me owning a game and giving it to subscribers at launch had started to work against each other. I think spreading Game Pass this widely is correct, because consoles are about to cost $100 or $150 more on August 1, depending on storage, and the July moves let players reach Game Pass without buying one. The risk is that ads and starter tiers teach people that Xbox games are a free sample, bundled into someone else's subscription or played an hour at a time, when Game Pass needs some of those people to decide it is worth paying for.

Content is why people subscribe, though, and Xbox is thinning its catalog just when it needs more reasons for people to pay. Xbox content and services revenue fell 5% from a year earlier in the quarter that ended March 31, and in April Microsoft guided to [a decline in the low teens for the June quarter](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/events/fy-2026/earnings-fy-2026-q3), which Microsoft said partly reflected the price cut. A drop in a quarter that ended before the April price cut could not have come from that cut, and since Sharma told staff in May that subscriber losses had sped up after the 2025 price and plan changes, it points to a demand problem alongside the margin problem, even though the same May memo said retention had improved since April. The departing studios may also take their next games elsewhere, and a promise that covers only publicly announced games, and only these reductions, leaves room to cancel projects nobody has seen. That case has weight, but I come down for the reset, because Sharma's figures describe a business that already had years of acquired studios behind its subscription and still earned a third to a tenth of the margins comparable businesses earn, which tells me more owned content had not fixed the margins, whatever the hardware costs added. I think weak demand is better answered by putting a catalog Xbox can afford in front of more people.

The case against buying small studios to fill Game Pass now comes from Sharma's own figures. I take her 64-cent line to cover only the studios she says Xbox isn't the best home for, and within that scope it says that in a typical year Xbox got back about 36 cents of every dollar it put into them. Since she doesn't say whether that counts what their games added to Game Pass, I read it as the case against owning that kind of studio to fill the catalog. What Xbox added to in July is reach, from Meta's subscription to Windows handhelds to free streaming with ads, and my guess is that the reset works only if some share of the people who find Xbox that way end up paying for Game Pass. If most stay on the free and starter versions, Microsoft will have traded studios for a bigger audience that pays less per person, and I don't think margins as thin as the ones Sharma described leave room for that.
