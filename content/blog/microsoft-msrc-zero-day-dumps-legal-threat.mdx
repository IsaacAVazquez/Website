# Microsoft Had the Better Argument on Zero-Day Dumps Until It Invoked the Digital Crimes Unit

I think the coverage of Microsoft's fight with an anonymous Windows researcher already made most of the argument I'd make. On May 27 the Microsoft Security Response Center (MSRC), the team outside researchers send Microsoft bugs to, [published a post](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/msrc/blog/2026/05/a-shared-responsibility-protecting-customers-through-coordinated-vulnerability-disclosure) about six zero-days an anonymous researcher had dumped online. It said Microsoft never got the details before they went public, that uncoordinated disclosures putting exploit code for unpatched bugs in attackers' hands are never justifiable, and that its Digital Crimes Unit would keep bringing cases against those attackers and anyone who enables their criminal activity. The next day The Register ran a story built mostly on security veterans criticizing that response, and none of them supported the dumps. Katie Moussouris, the Luta Security founder who started Microsoft's bug bounty program, [told The Register](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/05/28/microsoft-0-day-feud-escalates-as-researcher-threatens-another-windows-exploit-dump/5248085) that mentioning the Digital Crimes Unit made the post "vaguely threatening" and that Microsoft should be trying to avoid "the chilling effect on other researchers." When [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/29/microsoft-under-fire-for-threatening-security-researcher-with-criminal-investigation/) on May 29 that Microsoft was under fire, it quoted her again, warning of "a chilling effect of fewer people coming forward to report bugs." Microsoft is right that publishing working Windows exploits without warning puts its customers at risk, and wrong to answer it by invoking its Digital Crimes Unit, and Moussouris made the second half of that case. What I think the coverage underplayed is that Microsoft's own AI and bug bounty plans make that chill more expensive. With Microsoft expecting AI to keep turning up more bugs, its security depends more than ever on outside researchers choosing to report to it, and a legal threat aimed at one researcher gets read by all of them.

The harm is where I'd start, because it's real. The researcher, who goes by Nightmare Eclipse and Chaotic Eclipse, published six zero-days in Windows, Defender and BitLocker with exploit code on GitHub and GitLab, and [The Register reported the fourth and fifth on May 13](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/05/13/disgruntled-researcher-releases-two-more-microsoft-zero-days/5239758). Its May 28 story, citing the security firm Huntress, said attackers had exploited BlueHammer, RedSun and UnDefend, three of the six. A zero-day has no patch on the day it goes public, so posting one with working code gives attackers a head start on every company running the affected software. I joined Civitech as a QA analyst and built its testing process from scratch, and the instinct I took from that job is to side with whoever finds the bug, since a bug someone reports is one users don't hit first. This case strains that instinct. Microsoft says it saw none of these details before release, the risk landed on the people running Windows, and on the harm I think Microsoft is right.

The response is where I think Microsoft reached for the wrong tool. Read literally, the Digital Crimes Unit sentence is about criminals who exploit bugs like these and whoever enables them. But it sits in the same short paragraph as the line condemning uncoordinated disclosure, two sentences after it, in a post that names all six of the researcher's bugs, and it never says where enabling starts. TechCrunch read it as a threat of criminal investigation against the researcher, and that's the reading a careful researcher has to plan around. The same post says Microsoft works with hundreds of security researchers every year, and each of them has reason to wonder whether publishing working exploit code after a vendor misses a deadline would count. The warning also came from MSRC, the office that is supposed to be the safe place to send a bug. The day after the post, The Register reported that the researcher says Microsoft deleted the account they used to report bugs, which I have no way to check, and has promised another release on July 14.

What makes the tool choice expensive is what Microsoft said about AI earlier in the month. On May 12 it shipped [fixes for 137 vulnerabilities, 30 of them rated critical](https://www.theregister.com/patches/2026/05/13/doozy-of-a-patch-tuesday-includes-30-critical-microsoft-cves/5239224), and MSRC's Tom Gallagher [wrote](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/msrc/blog/2026/05/a-note-on-patch-tuesday) that releases would keep trending larger for some time, with more of that month's bugs found by Microsoft itself, many of them through AI. The same day it [introduced MDASH](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/05/12/defense-at-ai-speed-microsofts-new-multi-model-agentic-security-system-tops-leading-industry-benchmark/), a system that coordinates more than 100 AI agents across frontier models and smaller distilled ones, and said it found 16 of the month's 137. Microsoft argued that "the durable advantage lies in the agentic system around the model," and I think that's right, but frontier models are commercial products that outside researchers can build systems around too. In January 2025 I argued that [AI makes attackers and defenders more capable at the same time](/writing/cybersecurity-in-age-of-ai-software-engineer-perspective), and May shows what that looks like from inside a vendor. Microsoft has already put money behind outside discovery. On December 11, 2025, it [made every Microsoft online service eligible for bug bounties by default](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/msrc/blog/2025/12/in-scope-by-default), after awarding more than $17 million over the previous year. A company widening what outsiders can get paid to find, while saying its patch releases will keep growing, needs those outsiders to keep sending their bugs to it first, and I'd argue that makes researcher goodwill worth more to Microsoft than deterrence aimed at one person.

The strongest case for Microsoft starts with the literal text of the Digital Crimes Unit sentence and with the post's last line, which welcomes vulnerability reports from anyone through Microsoft's researcher portal, whatever their past dealings or reputation. Publishing working exploit code that attackers then use may well be a crime, and pursuing a crime alongside law enforcement is what a company should do. The researcher's complaint about the deleted account is one side's story. And if AI keeps making exploits faster to write, deterring the next person tempted to dump code could protect more customers than keeping a few researchers happy. I take all of that seriously, and I still land on the other side. The welcome covers reporting, and the post allows that Microsoft and researchers won't always agree, but the question researchers actually need answered is what happens to someone who reports, disagrees with how the report is handled, and then publishes, and the post doesn't say. Any civil suit or criminal referral can be handled quietly by Microsoft's lawyers and law enforcement without the security response team hinting at it. And deterrence only works on someone who is listening, and the July 14 promise suggests this researcher isn't.

My read of May is that cheap discovery changes what a vendor owes the people doing the discovering. If models and agents keep making serious bugs cheaper to find, as Microsoft's own May statements suggest, disclosure becomes a relationship a vendor has to keep up with everyone doing the finding. For Microsoft, the next time a researcher goes public, and this one has already named a date, I'd want the harm argument made as forcefully as it was on May 27, with any legal action handled quietly and kept out of MSRC's posts. I'd also want Microsoft to say where enabling starts, so a researcher who reports through the portal knows what conduct would put them on the wrong side of the Digital Crimes Unit, and to say what happened to the account the researcher says was deleted. That wouldn't soften Microsoft's position on uncoordinated dumps, and I think it would keep MSRC, the office researchers report to, from sounding like it also decides who gets pursued.
