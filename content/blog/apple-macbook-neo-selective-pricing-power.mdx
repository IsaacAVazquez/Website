# Apple Made the Entry Mac Cheap and Charged More a Step Up

Asus co-CEO S.Y. Hsu spent part of Asus's earnings call on an Apple laptop. As [9to5Mac reported on March 10](https://9to5mac.com/2026/03/10/asus-co-ceo-praises-macbook-neo-value-questions-its-productivity-firepower/), he said a budget-priced Mac had come as a shock to the whole PC industry, then described MacBook Neo as a content consumption device, similar to an iPad and short of what a mainstream notebook is used for, held back by 8GB of memory that can't be upgraded. The reviews in AppleInsider's roundup that day mostly read Neo as a set of well-chosen cuts, and the rest of Apple's first week of March was written up as price notes, with Engadget and 9to5Mac treating the higher Air and Pro prices as offset by doubled storage and MacRumors reading the Mac Studio's pricier memory as the DRAM shortage reaching Apple. I'd read all of it as one pricing decision that made the entry point to the Mac cheap and made the Air, the Pro, and the top Mac Studio memory more expensive, and a newcomer who skips Neo for an Air pays the higher price like anyone else. I think that's the right use of a memory shortage. Hsu's two points are the two halves of that bet, since the price brings new customers in and the 8GB decides whether they stay.

The memory shortage traces back to the data center build-out that, as I argued on March 1, is [carrying a large share of the economy's growth](/writing/ai-capex-vs-real-economy-2026). In January I wrote that [the AI boom was reaching Apple through its costs first](/writing/apple-record-quarter-ai-arrives-as-memory-costs), since higher memory costs, driven by companies building AI servers, were already in Apple's March-quarter margin guidance. On the January 29 call Cook also said Apple would [look at a range of options](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/01/29/apple-responds-to-rising-memory-chip-prices/) to deal with rising memory prices over the long term, if necessary. On March 10 [MacRumors relayed a TrendForce projection](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/03/10/apple-holds-edge-laptop-prices-increase/) that memory and processor costs could push mainstream laptop prices up nearly 40 percent in 2026, after DRAM and SSD rose from about 15 percent to more than 30 percent of the parts cost of a $900 laptop TrendForce modeled, and wrote that Apple's own chips insulate it from Intel's processor price increases but not from memory. Apple never tied March's prices to that remark, but March is the first place I think we can see what those options look like, and they differ across the lineup.

Neo is priced for people who don't own a Mac. Apple announced it on March 4 at [$599, or $499 for education](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/03/say-hello-to-macbook-neo/), with the A18 Pro, an iPhone chip, 8GB of memory, and 256GB of storage, and Apple named students, families, small business owners, and new Mac users as its audience. Two days earlier Apple announced the iPhone 17e at the same $599 with 256GB, which Apple said doubles the previous generation's entry storage at the same price. TrendForce, in a forecast MacRumors relayed on March 5, said Apple's silicon and standardized memory configurations let it undercut rivals as component costs rise, and forecast 4 to 5 million Neo shipments in calendar 2026. On March 20 Cook posted that "[Mac just had its best launch week ever for first-time Mac customers](https://9to5mac.com/2026/03/20/tim-cook-says-the-mac-just-set-a-new-launch-record-for-apple/)." The post covers Neo, the new Air, and the new MacBook Pros, which all shipped March 11, and it gives no unit or revenue figures, so I can't credit it to Neo alone. Still, the measure Apple chose to celebrate is new customers, which is what Neo is for.

One step up, the Air and the M5 Pro MacBook Pro each start about 10 percent higher, and the M5 MacBook Pro went from $1,499 to $1,699, about 13 percent, with 1TB in place of 512GB. The 13-inch MacBook Air with M5 starts at $1,099 with 512GB of storage, [$100 more than the M4 model's $999 with 256GB](https://www.engadget.com/computing/laptops/the-macbook-air-m5-starts-at-1099-up-100-from-the-m4-141612909.html), and the 14-inch MacBook Pro with M5 Pro starts at $2,199 with 1TB, $200 more than the M4 Pro model's $1,999 with 512GB, with [memory upgrade prices on both unchanged](https://9to5mac.com/2026/03/03/apple-macbook-price-increase-ram-same/). Whether that's an increase depends on the buyer, since someone who wanted 512GB gets it in the base Air and someone who didn't pays $100 more for storage they may not use. What I'd point to is the timing, because Apple raised the price of getting into the Air the same week it put a $599 laptop under it, so the price-sensitive buyer the $999 Air used to catch now has Neo. That's pricing power used carefully, with twice the storage to make it easier to accept.

At the high end Apple raised memory prices the most. [MacRumors reported on March 5](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/03/05/mac-studio-no-512gb-ram-upgrade/) that Apple had removed the 512GB memory option, previously a $4,000 upgrade, from the M3 Ultra Mac Studio and raised the 256GB upgrade from $1,600 to $2,000, with 256GB configurations quoting delivery into May. Apple didn't comment, and MacRumors tied the change to the DRAM shortage and to demand for machines that run AI models locally. That's 25 percent more for the same memory. I'd guess the people ordering a desktop with 256GB of memory are a small group running large models on their own hardware, and a buyer willing to wait until May has few places to take the business over $400.

The obvious pushback is that I'm reading strategy into cost accounting. On that view, Neo exists because Apple could reuse an iPhone chip and buy memory in standard configurations, the Air and Pro increases are costs passed through with storage added to soften them, and the Mac Studio change is a supply problem. I'd grant a good part of that, since TrendForce's cost figures explain why prices moved at all, and I'd grant Hsu's point about 8GB, because a first Mac that feels slow in its second or third year could lose Apple the customer it priced Neo to win. The pattern is where I disagree, because memory goes into everything Apple sells and Apple chose where the higher cost showed up, holding the entry iPhone at $599 with twice the storage and keeping memory upgrade prices on the Air and Pro flat, which pure cost pass-through would have raised. The Mac Studio increase fits either story, since that configuration carries the most memory, but the flat memory prices on the laptops don't fit pass-through alone. My own view, going back to a cross-functional pricing strategy I worked on at Civitech, is that the entry price decides who shows up, and Apple set the entry price of a Mac laptop at $599, $400 under the old Air, in the middle of a shortage.

March's prices put Apple at its cheapest where it wants new customers and most expensive where I'd guess its customers have the fewest other places to go, and I think that's the right way to handle a shortage its rivals are less equipped to absorb. The condition is the 8GB. Cook's post says the first half is working, for one launch week across three Macs and without a number, and the second half is whether people on a Mac with memory they can't upgrade still like it when they're ready for another. If they do, March is when Apple used a shortage to widen its base. If Neo feels slow before its owners want to replace it, Apple will have taught its new customers the lesson Hsu drew before it even shipped, that a $599 Mac with 8GB of memory is too limited to keep.
