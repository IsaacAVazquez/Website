# Microsoft Chose to Feed Its Own Copilots Before Azure, So Judge It on Copilot

Microsoft's January 28 earnings got graded on a single number. Azure and other cloud services [grew 39 percent](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2026/01/28/microsoft-cloud-and-ai-strength-drives-second-quarter-results-3/) in the December quarter, a point slower than the quarter before, though chief financial officer Amy Hood called it slightly ahead of Microsoft's own expectations, while capital expenditures including finance leases rose 66 percent from a year earlier to $37.5 billion, which the market read as more spending with no matching lift in growth. The stock fell about 10 percent on January 29, [its sharpest one-day drop since March 2020](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/29/microsoft-market-cap-earnings.html), and [Meta's shares rose the same day](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/29/meta-microsoft-stock-earnings-moves-tech.html) even though Meta guided to as much as $135 billion in 2026 capex. That grade leaves out what Hood told investors on the call, which is that the 39 percent was partly a choice. What I think that means is that Microsoft should be graded on whether Copilot usage has earned first claim on scarce compute, which it has only begun to show.

## The allocation order Hood described

Asked about the return on all that spending, Hood said it's probably better to think of Microsoft's Azure guidance as [an "allocated capacity guide,"](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/events/fy-2026/earnings-fy-2026-q2) and laid out the order, with new GPUs going first to Microsoft 365 Copilot and GitHub Copilot, then to R&D, and the remainder serving Azure customers. Then she quantified it, saying "if I had taken the GPUs that just came online in Q1 and Q2 ... and allocated them all to Azure, the KPI would have been over 40." Satya Nadella said Microsoft doesn't want to "maximize just one business" and wants to allocate capacity, while supply is short, to build "the best LTV portfolio," the most lifetime value across Azure and the Copilots.

I think that's a coherent strategy. If compute is the constraint, it's reasonable to send each new GPU where it expects the most value over the chip's life. But the disclosure changes what should be measured, because once Azure's growth rate is partly an output of Microsoft's own allocation, it stops working as a clean read on demand. Barclays' Raimo Lenschow said most investors had [judged the business on Azure growth alone](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/30/microsoft-stock-price-market-ai-cloud-azure-earnings.html), and he described Copilot as one of Microsoft's "higher-margin, first party offerings," which is the claim January's numbers had to support.

## What 15 million seats can carry

Microsoft did bring evidence. Nadella disclosed 15 million paid Microsoft 365 Copilot seats for the first time, said seat adds were a record, up more than 160 percent from a year earlier, and put GitHub Copilot at more than 4.7 million paid subscribers, up 75 percent year over year. He added that Microsoft 365 Copilot daily active users grew tenfold year over year. That's real growth, and GitHub's figure is the cleaner of the two, since it measures the subscriber base itself, while the 160 percent compares seats added in the last three months of 2025 with the same stretch of 2024.

I'd still call it thin support for first claim on the company's scarcest input. Fifteen million is at most about 3.3 percent of the more than 450 million paid Microsoft 365 commercial seats, and a paid seat is an upper bound on use, since a company can pay for licenses its employees rarely open. The usage figures that would close that gap came without a base, so tenfold growth could start from almost any number, and Microsoft gave no revenue, retention, or margin figure for Copilot. UBS analysts led by Karl Keirstead wrote that Microsoft 365 revenue growth ["is not accelerating due to Copilot"](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/29/microsoft-market-cap-earnings.html) and that "many checks on Copilot don't suggest a strong usage ramp." What that means is that Microsoft is allocating GPUs against usage levels it hasn't disclosed, and the seat count mostly tells us the bet has buyers.

A skeptic could push this further. Hood also said the GPU contracts Microsoft has described, including those for some of its largest customers, are sold for the entire useful life of the GPU, so an Azure GPU can carry a return Microsoft can count, and her own counterfactual says the GPUs sent to the Copilots and R&D would have found paying Azure customers. The "over 40" describes a quarter that didn't happen, so nobody outside Microsoft can check it, while the spending behind those GPUs is visible whichever way they were allocated, with roughly two-thirds of the quarter's capex on short-lived assets, primarily GPUs and CPUs, and Microsoft Cloud gross margin guided to roughly 65 percent for the March quarter, down from a year earlier, so the allocation swaps a return investors could have counted for one they can't see yet. I take that seriously, because the same allocation fits a long-term product bet and a convenient explanation for a slower cloud, and nothing in the January numbers separates those two stories.

## A backlog that leans on one customer

There's a third reading of the allocation that I think got too little attention. Microsoft's commercial remaining performance obligation, the contracted revenue it has yet to recognize, stood at $625 billion, up 110 percent from a year earlier, and Hood said [about 45 percent of it is from OpenAI](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/events/fy-2026/earnings-fy-2026-q2). By my arithmetic that's roughly $280 billion from one customer, leaving $340 billion to $350 billion from everyone else, a balance Hood said grew 28 percent. Evercore ISI analysts said [concerns about OpenAI's ability to meet funding commitments](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/29/meta-microsoft-stock-earnings-moves-tech.html) likely contributed to the selloff, though they called that sentiment "overblown."

Feeding Microsoft's own products first is a hedge as much as a growth bet, since the GPUs behind the Copilots support demand Microsoft owns outright, sold as seats and subscriptions it prices itself. It sits alongside what I wrote after Ignite in November, when Microsoft [put Anthropic's models next to OpenAI's across its products](/writing/microsoft-ignite-agent-365-governed-layer) and pitched enterprises on the governed layer around them, since that week I read the Anthropic deal as a platform decision first and a hedge second. I'd argue the hedge is sensible for a company with nearly half its backlog tied to one partner, and it raises the stakes on the first-party bet, since a hedge is only worth the demand it actually builds.

What January taught me about reading Microsoft is that the disclosure worth keeping is the allocation order, since the order in which a company spends its scarcest input is a claim about where it thinks the value is. Hood's order says a GPU serving a Copilot seat is worth more over its life than the same GPU rented through Azure, and that claim can be checked, just not with January's numbers. The checks run from active usage with a base attached, to Copilot revenue per seat, to renewal rates, to Microsoft 365 revenue growth picking up because of Copilot, the acceleration UBS says it isn't seeing. In December I wrote that [the capex bet was the part of 2026 I felt least able to time](/writing/2026-predictions-what-i-actually-believe), and I still can't time it, but the allocation order gives me a specific claim to check every quarter. If the Copilot numbers fill in, the 39 percent will read as a price Microsoft chose to pay, and if they don't, it will read as a miss against what investors wanted, with a good explanation attached.
