# Microsoft Can Bundle Copilot Into Microsoft 365 as Long as the Exit Stays Visible

On October 23 TechCrunch called Mico, the animated avatar in Copilot's voice mode, [a Clippy for the AI era](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/23/microsofts-mico-is-a-clippy-for-the-ai-era/), and called that day's Copilot Mode update in Edge [nearly identical to OpenAI's Atlas browser](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/23/two-days-after-openais-atlas-microsoft-launches-a-nearly-identical-ai-browser/), released two days earlier. Most of October's coverage of Microsoft's consumer AI news graded it that way, feature by feature and mostly against OpenAI. I think the bigger decision came on October 1, and it was about price. Earlier in October I wrote that OpenAI used DevDay to [turn ChatGPT into the place where other companies' software runs](/writing/openai-devday-2025-platform-play). Microsoft already owns places like that, and its consumer strategy is to put Copilot inside the subscriptions people already pay for. I'd argue that's the right distribution play, but the lawsuit Australia's consumer regulator filed on October 27 points to where it breaks, which is the moment a customer can't find the version without the new thing, and what that means is the plan without AI has to be as easy to see as the upsell.

## Premium replaces Copilot Pro

On October 1 Microsoft introduced [Microsoft 365 Premium](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/blog/2025/10/01/meet-microsoft-365-premium-your-ai-and-productivity-powerhouse/), a $19.99 a month plan in the US that shares the Word, Excel, PowerPoint, OneNote, and Outlook desktop apps and 1 TB of storage per person with up to six people, and gives the subscription owner Copilot built in, the Researcher and Analyst agents, and Microsoft's highest consumer Copilot limits. The same post said Microsoft would stop selling Copilot Pro, which had [launched in January 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/01/15/microsoft-launches-a-pro-plan-for-copilot/) at $20 a month per user and needed a Personal or Family plan to work inside the Office apps, and that it was raising the Copilot limits on Personal, at $9.99 a month, and Family, at $12.99, at no extra cost.

I think this is a smart simplification. Until October, the most AI a person could buy from Microsoft came as a $20 Copilot Pro charge stacked on a Microsoft 365 subscription, which invites people to add up two bills and drop one. Copilot has come with Personal and Family in the US since January 16, 2025, and as of October those plans get higher limits and the top tier is a single Microsoft 365 plan, Premium, so the most AI Microsoft sells now sits inside the subscription itself and stops being a second bill. What that does is finish turning Copilot from a separate charge into part of the reason to keep paying for Word and Excel.

## The Australian lawsuit

The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission's case is where that strategy gets tested, though it is only an allegation. [The regulator alleges](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/microsoft-in-court-for-allegedly-misleading-millions-of-australians-over-microsoft-365-subscriptions) that Microsoft misled about 2.7 million Australian Personal and Family subscribers after it integrated Copilot on October 31, 2024, when annual prices rose 45% for Personal, from A$109 to A$159, and 29% for Family, from A$139 to A$179. Its complaint is that Microsoft's communications about the increase left out the cheaper Classic plans without Copilot, which appeared only after a subscriber started to cancel. It is seeking penalties and redress in the Federal Court. The fairest point in Microsoft's favor is its own US announcement on January 16, 2025, which raised Personal and Family by $3 a month and said in its main text that existing subscribers could switch, [for a limited time](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/blog/2025/01/16/copilot-is-now-included-in-microsoft-365-personal-and-family/), to Classic plans without Copilot.

If the allegations hold, this is where bundling stops working as distribution and starts costing trust. A bundle works because most customers never have to think about it, and a visible exit is what makes that inertia fair. When the cheaper plan appears only once someone tries to leave, the customer learns the price notice left something out, and the next time Microsoft pairs an AI feature with a higher price, that customer reads the notice looking for what's missing.

Microsoft's best answer to my worry came on its [October 29 earnings call](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/events/fy-2026/earnings-fy-2026-q1). The company said Microsoft 365 Consumer subscriptions grew 7% from a year earlier to more than 90 million as of September 30, and that Microsoft 365 Consumer cloud revenue grew 26% year over year in the quarter. One $19.99 plan is easier to understand than an add-on stacked on a subscription, and I take seriously that most subscribers may simply want the AI included. But the revenue figure fits at least two stories. Microsoft credited the growth to higher average revenue per user, and the arithmetic agrees, since revenue growing 26% on 7% more subscriptions means most of the gain came from each subscription paying more, which is what you'd see if people valued Copilot enough to pay for it, and also what you'd see if people paid the higher price because they never saw the cheaper plan, as the regulator alleges in Australia. The earnings call can't tell those apart, but a visible exit would make them easier to tell apart, because once the plan without AI is easy to find, the people paying for Copilot are paying with the choice in front of them.

## Mico and the demand problem

I read the [Fall Release on October 23](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/23/microsofts-mico-is-a-clippy-for-the-ai-era/) as the other half of the bundle strategy. Mico is on by default in Copilot's voice mode, starting in the US, Canada, and the UK, alongside Groups, a shared Copilot chat, and Real Talk, a mode designed to push back on what the user says. Mustafa Suleyman, the CEO of Microsoft AI, said the company is "not chasing engagement or optimizing for screen time," and a mode built to disagree with you is an odd thing to ship if the goal is time in the app. I think these features are Microsoft trying to give people a reason to want what many of them already pay for. Microsoft's October 29 figure for the consumer Copilot app, nearly 50% more daily users than the prior quarter with no base given, covers July to September, before Mico or Groups existed, and says nothing about how many subscribers actually use Copilot inside Word or Excel. A default-on avatar comes from the same instinct as the bundle, and users can switch it off, so the same rule applies, and turning it off has to stay as easy as leaving it on. The demand has to come from people using Copilot, and pricing can't create that.

The pattern I'd pull out of October is that a bundle is a distribution decision and the visible exit is a trust decision, and Microsoft keeps the first only as long as it respects the second. Putting Copilot inside Personal, Family, and Premium gives it distribution inside a subscription business with more than 90 million subscriptions. What the bundle can't do on its own is show how many people want Copilot, because when the plan without AI is hard to find, nobody can tell a customer who chose Copilot from one who never saw the choice. If Copilot is as useful as Microsoft is betting, a visible exit should cost it very little, since few people will take it, and the revenue that stays will be revenue customers picked.
