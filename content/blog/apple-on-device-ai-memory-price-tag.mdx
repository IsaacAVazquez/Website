# Apple's On-Device AI Runs on Memory, and Now Memory Is on the Price Tag

I've already made my case about the Gemini side of WWDC, which is that [Apple caught up at the model layer by renting from Google](/writing/wwdc-2026-apple-ai-recap). The WWDC stories after that stayed on the Gemini question and on whether Siri AI is finally good. Apple's memory requirement got covered as a compatibility story, with [9to5Mac's June 8 write-up](https://9to5mac.com/2026/06/08/ios-27s-most-powerful-on-device-ai-requires-iphone-17-pro-iphone-air/) calling the base iPhone 17's absence from the list the biggest shock, and when Apple raised prices on Macs and iPads on June 25, [TechCrunch's headline](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/25/apple-raises-mac-and-ipad-prices-spares-iphone-for-now/) was about Apple sparing the iPhone. I think the memory requirement and the price increase belong in the same argument. Apple's on-device AI strategy is a memory strategy. June is when Apple, which by Tim Cook's account had been trying to shield customers from memory costs, started raising prices across the whole Mac and iPad lineup, after narrower increases to the Air and Pro entry prices in March, and I'd call that defensible for privacy and awkward for the entry products Apple was using to bring new people in.

Apple says [its most powerful on-device model](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/06/apple-introduces-siri-ai-a-profoundly-more-capable-and-personal-assistant/) is available on the iPhone Air, the iPhone 17 Pro and Pro Max, and iPads with M4 or later and Macs with M3 or later that have at least 12GB of memory. [Apple's model post](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/introducing-third-generation-of-apple-foundation-models) calls that model AFM 3 Core Advanced, a 20-billion-parameter model (parameters being a rough measure of size) with a sparse design that uses 1 to 4 billion of them at a time, and it says the full model is stored in flash storage, with a core of it kept in DRAM, the device's working memory, and other parts swapped in when a request needs them. By 9to5Mac's account Apple Intelligence needed 8GB, and Apple's best new on-device model needs 12GB, while Siri AI itself stays on the same hardware, down to the 8GB iPhone 15 Pro. Devices under the line, the MacBook Neo among them, still get Siri AI, and 9to5Mac expected them to reach many of the same features through Private Cloud Compute, the server system Apple uses for requests a device can't handle, only more slowly.

My verdict is that Apple made its best local AI a feature of its more expensive hardware. The version of Apple's privacy promise I find most convincing is the one where the work stays on the device, and Apple leans on it. Private Cloud Compute comes with its own promise that personal data is neither stored nor made accessible to Apple or anyone else. I take that seriously, but it's still a promise about servers, and the 12GB line decides how much of your AI has to lean on it. I think the line is honest engineering, since a bigger model needs more memory and Apple published its on-device model sizes, and it's also a new reason to buy a more expensive model.

On June 17, nine days after the keynote, Tim Cook told The Wall Street Journal that ["unfortunately, price increases are unavoidable."](https://9to5mac.com/2026/06/17/apple-confirms-price-increases-are-coming-to-its-products-due-to-ram-shortage/) He said Apple had been working to mitigate the huge increases being passed to it and to shield customers from them, and that the situation had become unsustainable. In January I argued that [the AI boom would reach Apple through its costs first](/writing/apple-record-quarter-ai-arrives-as-memory-costs), and Cook was describing the point where Apple can no longer keep the memory part of that inside its margin. He framed it as an industry problem and didn't connect it to how Apple builds its AI, so the connection is my own reading. What I think it means is that memory is a cost Apple's AI plans make harder to escape, since WWDC had just tied Apple's best on-device model to at least 12GB of it.

On June 25 Apple followed through. [MacRumors counted 14 products](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/06/25/apple-explains-why-it-raised-prices/) with increases ranging from $30 to $1,300, covering every Mac and iPad along with Apple TV, both HomePods, and Vision Pro, with the iPhone, Apple Watch, and AirPods left alone. Apple said AI data centers had created "an extraordinary surge in demand for memory and storage," the same two components, flash storage and DRAM, that Apple's model post says its best on-device model is stored in and runs from. TechCrunch's list of new prices took the MacBook Neo from $599 to $699, the 13-inch MacBook Air from $1,099 to $1,299, and the base iPad from $349 to $449.

Apple [launched the Neo in March](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/03/say-hello-to-macbook-neo/) at $599 with an iPhone chip, the A18 Pro, and 8GB of memory, and [I argued then](/writing/apple-macbook-neo-selective-pricing-power) that it was selective pricing power used well, cheap where Apple wanted new customers, and the right use of a memory shortage as long as the Neo was good enough to keep the people it brought in. The entry Mac got $100 more expensive for what Apple said were memory and storage costs, and its 8GB is still short of what Apple's best on-device AI needs. My verdict on the June 25 increases is that Apple put the cost it blamed on memory and storage onto the Mac and iPad and protected the iPhone, and that the Neo increase works against Apple's own reason for making the product.

The fairest argument against all of this is that memory is an industry-wide shock that says nothing about Apple's AI design. [Microsoft announced Xbox console price increases the same day](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/25/xbox-follows-apple-with-price-increases/), citing memory and storage costs more than 2.5 times their previous levels, without saying since when, which tells me the shortage reaches device makers whatever their AI plans are. The increases also hit Apple TV, both HomePods, and the 8GB Neo, none of which run the 12GB model, and a design built around the cloud would still need memory, just in Apple's data centers. And the iPhone, where I'd expect most people to meet Siri AI and where the 12GB line splits the lineup, didn't get more expensive. I'd grant all of that, and the iPhone decision softens my argument more than anything else here. Where I'd push back is that Apple's design decides how much memory a device needs to run its best AI locally, and every device Apple wants to sell with that ability has to carry at least 12GB bought at 2026 prices, so part of Apple's exposure to the shortage is a choice it made about where its AI runs.

Since the June 25 increases, Apple's privacy design and its prices have depended on the same component. Memory decides how much of Siri AI runs on your own device, and memory is what AI data centers are bidding up, so the part of Apple's AI I find easiest to defend on privacy is also the part that makes its hardware more expensive to build. For the first-time buyer the Neo was built for, $699 is a different decision from $599, and it buys a Mac that sits below Apple's own line for its best on-device model. I'd guess some of the people March was meant to bring in will stop at the price before they ever hear Apple's argument about where their data is processed, and I don't think the privacy case does much for a buyer who was there for the price in the first place.
