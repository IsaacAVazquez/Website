# Apple Is Turning Live Sports into a Retention Feature for the Bundle

The Apple TV headline that stuck with me ran on 9to5Mac on November 14, [relaying a Sportico report](https://9to5mac.com/2025/11/14/apples-mls-deal-revised-to-end-in-2029-three-years-early-report/) that Apple's MLS deal would now end in 2029, three years early. On its own, that reads like Apple backing away from a bet that didn't pay off. Next to what Apple announced the day before and the month before, I think it points the other way. On November 13 Apple and MLS said [every MLS match would come with an Apple TV subscription at no extra cost starting in 2026](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2025/11/major-league-soccer-is-coming-to-apple-tv-starting-in-2026/), and on October 17 Apple said it would [become the exclusive US broadcaster of Formula 1](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2025/10/apple-is-the-exclusive-new-broadcast-partner-for-formula-1-in-the-us/) for five years from 2026, with F1 TV Premium, the sport's own streaming service, included for subscribers. Apple is making live sports a feature of Apple TV itself, a reason to keep paying month to month, which I think is right for a service with no ad tier. It also means the deals should be judged on churn, the share of subscribers who cancel each month, and on how many sports viewers end up paying for Apple's wider bundle, and Apple gives outsiders no way to check either one.

The November 13 announcement is the plainest version of the trade Apple is making. Since 2023, following a full MLS season on Apple has mostly meant buying Season Pass, a standalone subscription with its own price. From 2026, every match comes with the base subscription, Season Pass ends after the 2025 season, and an Apple TV subscription will be part of clubs' 2026 full-season ticket packages. Apple gave up a product with its own revenue line to make Apple TV itself worth keeping. A league season puts something new on the schedule almost every week for most of the year, and I'd guess plenty of people subscribe for a show, finish it, and cancel. The ticket packages, like the Formula 1 races and practice sessions Apple says will be free in the app, also look like ways to sign people up, so new subscribers among sports viewers is a second number worth watching. I still think retention decides these deals, since a fan who cancels when the season ends pays for a few months of a contract that costs Apple every year. Eddy Cue, Apple's services chief, told Screen International that there are [no plans for an ad-supported tier](https://9to5mac.com/2025/11/07/apple-tv-execs-talk-2026-content-lineup-ad-supported-plans-and-more/), which leaves the subscription as the main way these rights earn their money.

The Sportico report is the harder fact, and I don't want to explain it away. Sportico's sources said the contract, which [SportsPro described on November 17 as a ten-year, $2.5 billion global deal](https://www.sportspro.com/news/mls-apple-tv-broadcast-deal-season-pass-contract-november-2025/), will end after the 2028 to 2029 season instead of running through 2032. The likeliest reading is that the standalone model didn't perform the way Apple and MLS hoped. The same reporting adds two conditions that complicate it. Apple gave up an option it already had to walk away after the 2027 season, and it will pay MLS about $50 million more through June 2029 than the old schedule would have, because the old deal saved its largest payments for the later years. So Apple is committing to MLS for about eighteen months longer than it had to while skipping the most expensive stretch, and I find that hard to square with a simple exit. Apple may still have paid too much for MLS, which nobody outside the two companies can rule out, but the revision can be read two ways. One is that Apple is getting out of sports, and the other is that Apple is getting out of selling sports as a separate product, and what separates them is what Apple did in the same few weeks. A company leaving sports doesn't take five years of exclusive US Formula 1 rights and make its subscription the only way to get F1 TV Premium in the US from 2026. I read the MLS revision as a retreat from the separate price tag.

The rename belongs to the same decision. Apple TV+ became plain Apple TV in [a single sentence near the end of an October 13 press release](https://www.apple.com/tv-pr/news/2025/10/apple-original-films-blockbuster-feature-f1-the-movie-from-joseph-kosinski-to-make-global-streaming-debut-on-friday-december-12-2025/) about F1 The Movie, so the service, app, and hardware share a name. On November 20 Apple said Pluribus had become [its most successful new drama series debut ever](https://9to5mac.com/2025/11/20/pluribus-apple-tv-drama-record/) and shared no viewer numbers. It is the same move I wrote about after the iPhone 17 event in September, when Apple put its stage time into the hardware it leads on and [said as little as possible about the AI it was behind on](/writing/apple-iphone-17-ai-strategy-2025), since in both cases Apple chooses what not to say. Apple is building a subscription whose value depends on whether people stay, and it has chosen not to publish the measure that would show whether they do.

The strongest objection is that bundling is convenient for a company that won't publish numbers, since once everything sits inside one subscription, nobody outside Apple can say whether MLS earned its cost. SportsPro wrote that the missing viewing figures have been the weakest part of the contract, and I think that's fair. Where I land differently is on what these deals should be judged by. An ad-supported network sells its audience to advertisers, which is why ratings get published. Apple mostly earns its money on these rights when someone subscribes and keeps subscribing, so for this service retention is the right way to judge a sports deal, even though only Apple can measure it well. Keeping that number private still has a cost, since every contract change gets read in the least flattering way, which is how the November 14 headline landed.

I'd score these deals on a number Apple can calculate from its own data, monthly churn among subscribers who watch MLS or Formula 1 once both sit in the base subscription in 2026, compared with subscribers who don't. Sports fans might stay longer anyway, so it wouldn't be a clean test, but it would say more than a record with no figure attached. Failing that, Apple could publish the share of sports viewers who pay for Apple One, the bundle of Apple's subscriptions. Either would tell us something about Services, which brought in $28.75 billion in the quarter that ended September 27. By my math from [Apple's statements](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/pdfs/fy2025-q4/FY25_Q4_Consolidated_Financial_Statements.pdf), Services ran at about a 75 percent gross margin that quarter against about 36 percent for products. Apple doesn't break Apple TV out of Services, so I'm not claiming the service earns anything like those margins, only that sports are there to keep people paying for Apple TV and, through Apple One, for the highest margin part of Apple, and if the same kinds of subscribers cancel less once MLS and Formula 1 join the base plan, allowing for a North American World Cup and the December streaming debut of F1 The Movie, Apple has a real answer to the claim that it is getting out of sports, though not to whether it paid too much for MLS. Until it publishes something like that, I'd treat the records as marketing and the MLS revision as a partial retreat, and I'd still bet the structure is right.
