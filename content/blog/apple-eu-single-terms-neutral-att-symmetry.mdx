# Apple's European Concessions Come Down to One Rulebook for Everyone

Apple's July 31 quarterly filing carried a line, which the Financial Times picked up on August 18, warning that on some sales through alternative payment or distribution channels, Apple ["may not earn a commission at all."](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/320193/000032019326000020/aapl-20260627.htm) The FT paired it with a third-party [Appfigures estimate](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/08/18/apple-app-store-revenue-falling/) that Apple's US App Store commission revenue had fallen 18 percent between the start of 2026 and mid-August, and the coverage read August as courts and regulators eroding the commission. I don't dispute the pressure, but I think that reading misses what Apple did with it. Apple's two European concessions that week, a neutral tracking prompt agreed with Germany's competition regulator on August 17 and one set of terms for every EU developer announced the next day, both accept symmetry, meaning Apple's rules will apply the same way to everyone they touch, every developer under the terms and Apple's own apps under the tracking prompt, and I think accepting that was right, because one published rulebook is a better product for developers than years of exceptions set by courts and regulators.

## One set of terms for every developer in the EU

Apple's [August 18 announcement](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/08/apple-announces-changes-for-apps-in-the-european-union/) puts every developer distributing apps in the EU on the same terms from October 1, with commissions replacing the Core Technology Fee and two other charges. App Store sales through Apple's in-app purchase pay 26 percent, sales through another payment processor 20 percent, and purchases through links to the web 15 percent. What Apple says is the vast majority of developers, including its small business and partner programs, pays 15, 10, and 10 percent instead, and so do subscriptions after their first year. Apps distributed outside the App Store pay a 5 percent Core Technology Commission on digital sales. The European Commission welcomed the terms and said it would monitor them, without saying the matter was closed.

What I like is that a developer can read the schedule once and know what each channel costs, from Apple's payment system, to its own processor, to a web link, to leaving the store, and pick one on price. The pressure came from Brussels, but the shape, one schedule with the rate set by the channel, is one Apple has now reused with three regulators, since the terms it set under Japan's new law in December 2025 and its Brazil agreement in June already used the same 15 percent rate on web links and 5 percent Core Technology Commission. In May I argued that Apple [defends the openness it controls and opposes the openness it doesn't](/writing/apple-siri-switchboard-ai-openness-on-its-terms), and that publishing the rules for its Siri switchboard would make its case easier to believe. A published fee schedule for every EU developer is the same kind of move applied to the App Store, and I think it's a start.

## A tracking prompt that binds Apple too

On August 17 Apple [agreed to commitments](https://9to5mac.com/2026/08/17/apple-will-comply-with-fairer-app-tracking-transparency-rules-in-the-eu/) with Germany's Federal Cartel Office, which had found that Apple held other developers to stricter App Tracking Transparency rules than its own apps. ATT is the prompt that asks whether an app can track you across other companies' apps and websites. Apple will make it neutral in wording and design and let developers fold Apple's request into their own consent prompt, with four months to make the change and seven years of monitoring after that. When the office began testing the offer in December 2025, its president, Andreas Mundt, said Apple had agreed to neutral prompts for its own services as well as other developers' apps. That addresses the double consent [Italy's competition authority fined Apple €98.6 million over](https://en.agcm.it/en/media/press-releases/2025/12/A561) in December 2025, saying Apple should have let developers get consent in a single step, a fine Apple said it would appeal.

Apple warned in October 2025 that lobbying might force it to withdraw ATT for European users, which I folded into my argument that [Apple's privacy line moves depending on who is asking](/writing/apple-privacy-line-moves-with-who-is-asking). What I think August shows is that ATT gets to stay because Apple accepted that it has to bind Apple too. A tracking prompt that asks more of other developers than of the company showing it is easy to attack as anticompetitive, and one that treats Apple's apps the same way is much easier to defend as privacy.

I can see the case that calling either concession a choice flatters Apple. On July 8 the EU's General Court [upheld Apple's gatekeeper designation](https://curia.europa.eu/site/upload/docs/application/pdf/2026-07/cp260096en.pdf) under the Digital Markets Act, the EU's rules for the largest platforms. Regulators in France, Italy, and Germany had all acted on ATT, so these concessions are what that pressure produced. Epic called the new charges junk fees that violate the DMA, and a 5 percent commission on apps that never touch the App Store is hard to call payment for a service those developers chose. Those points are fair, and I expect the Core Technology Commission to stay contested. I still think one schedule was a decision with consequences, because Apple could have answered each ruling with another carve-out, and from October 1 every EU developer plans around one table that Apple has to defend in public.

## The US link-out fight

In December 2025 the Ninth Circuit upheld the finding that Apple was in contempt of the injunction in Epic Games' case, [agreeing that its 27 percent commission](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2025/12/11/25-2935.pdf) on purchases through links out of apps had a prohibitive effect, and said Apple may charge a fee once the district court approves one. Apple has collected nothing on US link-outs since April 2025. On June 30 the Supreme Court agreed to review the contempt standard and declined to hear Apple's argument that relief should apply only to Epic. After the district judge refused on August 11 to pause the fee proceedings, Apple [proposed on August 13](https://9to5mac.com/2026/08/13/apple-proposes-commissions-of-up-to-15-for-off-app-store-purchases-in-the-us/) a 15 percent fee for standard apps, 10 percent for some partner programs and subscription renewals, and 5 percent for its Small Business Program, and asked for a settlement conference. Epic said the Ninth Circuit's standard points to zero and, on August 17, that any deal has to benefit all developers.

I think the European template is the one to carry over. The proposed tiers already read like a schedule, and Epic's condition is the symmetry Apple accepted in Europe and argued against when it asked the Supreme Court to limit relief to Epic. Apple has a real legal question before the Court, and I'd still settle, since as long as the contempt appeal and the fee proceedings both stay open, no US developer knows what a link will cost.

The trade Apple made in August was to give up a looser tracking prompt for its own apps and a menu of separate terms for different developers, and in exchange keep control of writing the rules. In Europe that bought it ATT and a commission on every channel, down to 5 percent outside its store. The US version would be one published link-out schedule for every developer, with each rate tied to what the Ninth Circuit said a link fee can cover, meaning the necessary costs plus some compensation for Apple's intellectual property, reached through the settlement talks Apple requested on August 13. Apple would give up whatever bargaining power a Supreme Court win might hand back and get a rulebook it wrote and has to follow, which is the price it paid in Europe to keep the platform its own.
