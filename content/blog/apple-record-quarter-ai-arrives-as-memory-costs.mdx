# Apple's Best iPhone Quarter Shows AI Hitting It Through Costs First

Alphabet passed Apple in market value in early January for the first time since 2019, and [Yahoo Finance's January 8 story](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/alphabet-overtakes-apple-as-worlds-second-most-valuable-company-behind-nvidia-154751861.html) read the change as a verdict on AI. It put the two companies at $3.94 trillion and $3.84 trillion and explained the gap with Gemini 3's benchmark results, a run of executive departures, and an Apple that had yet to prove itself as an AI leader with its next Siri still to come. Three weeks later, on January 29, Apple reported [the best iPhone quarter it has ever had](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/01/apple-reports-first-quarter-results/), and I read the results as complicating that verdict. iPhone revenue set a record before the new Siri, the thing that story was waiting on, had shipped, and the AI boom reached Apple anyway, first through its costs, since the guidance for the March quarter already includes a slightly larger hit from memory chips that, as MacRumors put it in its write-up of the call, companies building AI servers are bidding up.

The demand case is the easiest one to read. Revenue for the quarter that ended December 27 was [$143.8 billion, up 16 percent](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/pdfs/fy2026-q1/FY26_Q1_Consolidated_Financial_Statements.pdf), and iPhone brought in $85.3 billion of it, up 23 percent from $69.1 billion a year earlier and about 59 percent of the total. Greater China sales rose 38 percent to $25.5 billion, and [Counterpoint, which counts shipments, credited the iPhone 17 lineup](https://9to5mac.com/2026/01/20/apple-counters-china-market-slump-as-iphone-shipments-jump-in-q4-2025-report/) with making Apple the top vendor by shipments in China's smartphone market in the fourth quarter, with a 21.8 percent share and shipments up 28 percent in a market that shrank 1.6 percent. On [the earnings call](https://sixcolors.com/post/2026/01/this-is-tim-complete-transcript-of-apples-q1-2026-financial-call/), Cook called demand staggering, said channel inventory ended the quarter very lean, and said factory capacity for the three-nanometer process Apple's newest chips are made on is limiting supply in the March quarter.

I'd be careful about reading a single story into the 23 percent, because it fits more than one. It could be a China rebound, since Greater China grew faster than any other region, or it could be buyers moving up to Pro models, and the headline figure doesn't say which. What none of those explanations needs is the more personalized Siri, which Apple delayed in 2025 and still had not shipped when the quarter closed. Cook did say most people on iPhones that can run Apple Intelligence actively use it, without giving a number, but using a feature and buying a phone for it are different things. It's also possible AI features matter more for the next upgrade cycle than for this one, so I'd limit what this quarter shows to the phones Apple sold in it. My verdict on demand is that the iPhone 17 cycle is selling on hardware, which fits the near-term half of what [I argued in September](/writing/apple-iphone-17-ai-strategy-2025), that being the best at hardware still wins for now and gets riskier every year.

The margin case is where I think AI actually shows up, and it's also where the evidence against me is strongest. Gross margin was 48.2 percent, up from 46.9 percent a year earlier, and when I work it out from [Apple's statements](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/pdfs/fy2026-q1/FY26_Q1_Consolidated_Financial_Statements.pdf), Services earned about a 76.5 percent gross margin on $30.0 billion of sales against about 40.7 percent on $113.7 billion of products, so Services were about 21 percent of revenue and about 33 percent of gross margin dollars. For the March quarter, Apple guided to revenue growth of 13 to 16 percent, gross margin of 48 to 49 percent, and operating expenses of $18.4 to $18.7 billion. Cook said memory had a minimal effect on December-quarter margin, that it would weigh a little more in the March quarter, that the forecast already includes that, and that market prices for memory keep rising significantly.

That forecast is the best argument against my thesis. Margin went up, Apple guided to a range centered slightly above 48.2 percent with the memory cost already counted, and the company absorbed tariff costs that CFO Kevan Parekh said landed roughly at the $1.4 billion Apple had projected for the December quarter while still expanding margin. Parekh credited that expansion to a strong Pro mix and the lift a launch quarter gives, so it's real evidence that Apple can take a cost increase, though a mix quarter flatters it. So my read on margin is a narrow one. The margins in this report are strong, and the memory cost Cook folded into the March-quarter forecast is the signal I'd watch, because memory sits inside every iPhone, Mac, and iPad and so lands mostly on the products side, where gross margin is roughly half what Services earns.

The third case is where Apple's own AI money goes. Research and development rose 32 percent to $10.9 billion, about 7.6 percent of revenue and roughly twice the pace of sales growth, though Apple doesn't say how much of that is AI. Payments for property, plant, and equipment, which cover tooling, facilities, retail stores, and data centers, were $2.4 billion, down from $2.9 billion a year earlier, though Parekh said the year-earlier quarter included the build-out of Apple's Private Cloud Compute servers and that it's hard to draw conclusions from the capex line. For the model itself, Apple and Google said on January 12 that [the next generation of Apple Foundation Models will be based on Google's Gemini models and cloud technology](https://blog.google/company-news/inside-google/company-announcements/joint-statement-google-apple/) under a multi-year collaboration, and Cook declined on the call to share the terms. What that means is that whatever Apple spends on its own AI sits in a research line that doesn't break out AI and a partner deal whose terms it won't share, and the December-quarter drop in capital spending can't settle the question either way.

Memory is the place where other companies' AI spending turns into a cost for Apple. MacRumors, writing up the call, said [prices for RAM and NAND, the working memory and flash storage inside every device, are surging because of demand from companies building out AI servers](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/01/29/apple-responds-to-rising-memory-chip-prices/). I'd argue the AI spending Apple has the least control over belongs to other companies, because Apple sets its own AI budget, from the research line to the Gemini deal, and it doesn't set memory prices.

Apple's exposure to the AI boom this year runs through its suppliers, and this quarter says the boom can reach a company through a cost it doesn't control well before it reaches it through the product everyone is waiting on. My guess is that margins land inside the 48 to 49 percent forecast for the March quarter, though Apple's own explanation for the steady guide is a seasonal shift toward Services and mix, not buying power, and the products margin near 41 percent is a December-quarter high from a launch quarter. That makes the products margin the line that will show whether memory is actually getting absorbed. How long it holds after that depends on how long memory prices keep climbing, and that is being driven by how much other companies keep spending on AI servers.
