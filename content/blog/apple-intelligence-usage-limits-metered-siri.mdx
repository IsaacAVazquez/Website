# The iPhone Price Won't Cover All of Siri AI

John Ternus opened his first keynote as Apple's CEO on September 9 by [arguing that no product is better suited than the iPhone to be a person's AI hub](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/09/apple-ceo-john-ternus-says-the-best-ai-device-is-still-the-iphone/), and he leaned on privacy and on-device processing to make it. The stories that followed were mostly about price. The iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max [start at $1,199 and $1,299 for 256GB](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/09/apple-debuts-iphone-18-pro-and-iphone-18-pro-max/), each $100 above last year's launch prices, and [the 1TB models are $300 above last year's](https://9to5mac.com/2026/09/16/iphone-18-pro-review/). The older iPhones Apple kept selling went up $100 each, and Apple's first foldable, iPhone Duo, starts at $1,999. A support document Apple posted the same day got far less of that attention, and I think it says more about what an iPhone is now. It says Apple will put usage limits on Siri AI and other Apple Intelligence features that run in Apple's cloud, and that more access will cost a fee. I think the line Apple drew, between the phone and the cloud, is the right one, but it puts the assistant itself outside what the purchase fully includes in the same month the purchase got more expensive, and it puts more of the job of being a complete assistant on the models that run on the phone.

## What the September 9 page says

A smaller version of this was already in Apple's WWDC announcement on June 8, which said [some Apple Intelligence features, image generation among them, have daily usage limits because they rely on powerful server models](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/06/apple-unveils-next-generation-of-apple-intelligence-siri-ai-and-more/), and that most iCloud+ plans include more access. The [September 9 support document](https://support.apple.com/en-us/127901) goes further. It lists server-side features, among them Siri AI, photo tools like Clean Up, Image Playground, Apple's cloud models in Shortcuts, and third-party apps that call those models, and says Apple will put usage limits on these and other Apple Intelligence features in the future. Then it adds, "Increased access to such features will be available for a fee." As of September 26, Apple had published no limit numbers, and it had not said what the fee for more Siri AI access would cost or which plan would carry it. My read is that Apple Intelligence is getting a ceiling the purchase price doesn't cover, and the September 9 page is the first Apple document I found that puts the assistant itself, the headline feature of iOS 27, under those coming limits in writing.

## Where Apple drew the line

The limits apply to what runs in Private Cloud Compute, the server system, partly hosted on Google Cloud, that Apple uses for requests a phone can't handle on its own. The support page frames the limits around server-side models, though its list is open-ended, so the models that run on the phone, including the most capable one, which Apple tied in June to devices with at least 12GB of memory, appear to stay inside what the buyer paid for. That's the half I wrote about in June, when I argued that [Apple's on-device AI is a memory strategy whose cost had moved onto the price tag](/writing/apple-on-device-ai-memory-price-tag). Apple's earlier statements point to iCloud+ as the way to buy more. Asked about AI compute costs on the July 30 earnings call, Tim Cook, then still CEO, [said people who use Siri AI a lot can move up on an iCloud plan](https://9to5mac.com/2026/07/30/siri-ai-may-require-a-paid-subscription-for-heavy-users/), while calling the balance uncertain, and in August iOS 27's fifth beta added a prompt offering more Apple Intelligence through an upgraded iCloud+ plan, with higher tiers raising the limits. Developers get a different deal, since those in the App Store Small Business Program with fewer than 2 million total first-time App Store downloads can [call Apple's models on Private Cloud Compute at no cloud API cost](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/06/apple-aids-app-development-with-new-intelligence-frameworks-and-advanced-tools/). What that means is that Apple is choosing who pays for its cloud time, with small developers paying nothing for their apps' requests and users getting an allowance with paid tiers above it.

The reason I think the line belongs between the phone and the data center is that a one-time purchase can pay for a model running on hardware the buyer owns, while every request sent to Apple's cloud costs Apple something, and a heavy user's requests have no natural limit. I made a version of this argument about agents in May, that [the cost of running them sits in a small share of heavy runs that an average hides](/writing/what-an-ai-agent-actually-costs-in-production), and that matters here because the price of a phone is set once, for every kind of user, so it can't cover what the heaviest users cost Apple's cloud.

## The objection that buyers pay twice

The complaint I'd expect from buyers is that they now pay twice. A Pro buyer pays $100 more up front than last year's buyer did, $300 more at 1TB, and heavier use of the Apple Intelligence Ternus put at the center of his case for the iPhone will cost extra once Apple sets the limits. The fee would also come from a company that agreed to pay $250 million, without admitting wrongdoing, to settle a class action claiming it advertised Siri features that didn't ship on time, and [claims opened September 21](https://9to5mac.com/2026/09/21/you-can-now-claim-your-payout-from-apples-250-million-siri-settlement/), a week after iOS 27's public release on September 14 brought Siri AI out as a beta. That history gives buyers some reason for skepticism when Apple describes what a phone includes. I'd argue the objection is strongest on the Pro, where someone paying $1,199 or more would reasonably expect the whole feature set. My answer is that the two charges pay for different things, the phone and the models on it in one case and time in Apple's cloud in the other. That answer holds up only if the part the purchase still pays for works as a complete assistant, meaning the models on the phone handle most of what people ask Siri day to day. Rivals may also keep comparable features free to win people over, which would make Apple's allowance look stingy.

There's a fair case that I'm reading too much into a support page. Apple hasn't said where the limits sit, and they may rarely bind for typical users, in which case the fee mostly guards against heavy or automated use. I'd concede both points. Even so, a limit most people never hit still changes what the purchase includes, since the buyer will get a phone plus an allowance whose size Apple sets, and anything past it will be sold separately.

The change in September that I think will matter longest is that Apple put in writing that the assistant itself will fall under limits the purchase doesn't cover. For the purchase to still feel complete, the models on the phone probably have to carry the everyday work of the assistant, from questions about someone's own messages, mail, and photos, to actions inside apps, to the quick requests people make without thinking about where they're processed, so that the allowance only matters to someone doing something unusual. I think Ternus's pitch for the iPhone has to do more work after September 9, because the on-device part is the part the buyer has already paid for. If the phone handles the ordinary requests well, I'd guess the fee reads as an extra for heavy users, and if it doesn't, the allowance is probably what people notice first, which is a hard thing to explain on a phone whose starting price just went up $100.
