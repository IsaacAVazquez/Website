# Anthropic's IPO Pitch Leaves Public Buyers the Prospectus and Little Else

On August 18 The Information reported that Anthropic was preparing shares with extra votes for its founders, days after reports of a possible $2 trillion valuation and a $65 billion run-rate. The coverage mostly treated the founder shares as a step on the way to a record listing. I'm willing to grant that the business is as strong as the numbers say. What I think a public buyer should read first is the governance, since founder votes stacked on a trust that is on track to elect most of the board leave outside shareholders little say over governance, and that makes the prospectus the main accountability mechanism they will get.

## The investor documents

Documents shown to prospective IPO investors and seen by Bloomberg put [second-quarter revenue above $11.5 billion](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-14/anthropic-revenue-ahead-of-ipo-surges-over-14-fold-in-second-quarter), more than 14 times the $787 million a year earlier and more than double the first quarter's $4.73 billion, with positive adjusted operating income. Bloomberg also reported, and Reuters reported the same figure, that the run-rate reached $65 billion at the end of July, up from $47 billion in May and about $9 billion at the end of 2025. I'd attach three caveats. The figures are preliminary, and Anthropic declined to comment. Adjusted operating income is profit on terms the company sets itself, and SEC rules require any filing that uses an adjusted measure to show the closest standard accounting measure with at least equal prominence and reconcile the two. And the $65 billion is a run-rate, a recent stretch of revenue projected over a full year, which during growth this fast sits above the revenue the company has actually booked.

## The $2 trillion pitch

Six backers told the FT that Anthropic [could seek $2 trillion or more](https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2026/anthropic-could-seek-2-trillion-valuation-in-record-ipo/), about double its $965 billion valuation from May. They expect annualized revenue of $100 billion to $120 billion by the end of 2026, and one argued that a company growing this fast should trade at no less than 30 times revenue, which on that projection is $3 trillion to $3.6 trillion. Thirty times the July run-rate is about $1.95 trillion, and thirty times the second quarter annualized, about $46 billion, is about $1.4 trillion, so the $2 trillion target is roughly 31 times one figure and 43 times the other, and it only looks cheap against revenue nobody has booked yet. On August 25 the Wall Street Journal reported that Anthropic would tell investors its [addressable market exceeds $30 trillion](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/anthropic-pitches-ipo-investors-30-172107288.html), defined by the value of work its models could do. I read that as a statement of ambition that no revenue line can be checked against, and the $2 trillion as a number only the buyers at the listing can confirm.

## Founder votes and the trust

The governance is where public buyers come out worst. Reuters followed The Information in reporting that Anthropic was [preparing a class of stock with extra votes](https://money.usnews.com/investing/news/articles/2026-08-18/anthropic-prepares-supervoting-power-for-founders-ahead-of-ipo-the-information-reports) for Dario Amodei and his six co-founders to insulate them from outside shareholder pressure, though the vote ratio wasn't disclosed and the structure could still change. It would sit on top of the Long-Term Benefit Trust, which under a 2023 design holds shares that let it [elect and remove directors on a schedule of time and funding milestones](https://www.anthropic.com/news/the-long-term-benefit-trust), with a majority of the board within four years. The founders would hold extra votes and the trust would be on its way to choosing most of the board, so public buyers would get the economics without control over either the founders or the board.

The best case for this structure is strong. As a founder-led private company, Anthropic refused to let the Defense Department, which the administration calls the Department of War, use Claude for autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance, and on August 27 a federal judge [ruled unlawful one of the two supply chain risk designations](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/anthropic-pentagon-blacklist-claude-judge-rcna594825) the Pentagon imposed afterward, with the other still before the D.C. Circuit. Google listed in 2004 with ten-vote shares for its founders and early holders, Delaware law makes the directors of a public benefit corporation like Anthropic balance stockholders' financial interests with its stated public benefit, the trust checks the founders, and nobody has to buy. I accept most of that, and the structure does protect the mission. But a mission shielded from shareholder votes is exactly why disclosure has to carry the accountability the votes can't. [Google's prospectus told buyers plainly](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1288776/000119312504073639/ds1.htm) that they would get the full economic upside and less say over strategy than shareholders at most public companies. The prospectus also carries legal liability, since Section 11 of the Securities Act lets buyers sue the company and its directors over a material misstatement or omission in the registration statement it belongs to.

## The compute obligations

The disclosure I'd weigh most is what Anthropic has agreed to pay for compute. In April I read the gap between what Amazon and Google paid for Anthropic stock and what venture investors offered as a sign that [compute was the input Anthropic was shortest on](/writing/anthropic-strategic-investors-paid-half-vc-price), and the deals since July fit that read, though twenty-year leases also fit a company locking in capacity ahead of demand it expects. In July TeraWulf announced a 20-year Anthropic lease for about 401 megawatts in Kentucky, worth about $19 billion by its estimate. On August 10 Riot Platforms disclosed a $9.1 billion, 20-year Texas lease with a tenant Bloomberg reported was Anthropic, which neither company confirmed. On August 26 Bloomberg and TechCrunch reported that Anthropic [agreed to pay Nscale $45 billion over six years](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/26/anthropic-continues-compute-gobbling-streak-in-45-billion-deal-with-nscale/) for Nvidia capacity starting in late 2027, on top of the more than $100 billion over ten years it committed to AWS in April.

Leasing through partners is a sensible way to scale, and it's also why the obligations have to be printed in full, since they run six to twenty years and the revenue meant to cover them grew from $787 million a quarter to over $11.5 billion in one year. In June I argued that the fragile part of the AI trade was [the gap between AI spending and the revenue it has produced](/writing/is-the-ai-mega-cap-rally-a-bubble), and Anthropic's prospectus could show public buyers both sides of that gap in one document.

What connects the four for me is that each part of this listing, from the run-rate, to the multiples its backers are quoting, to the twenty-year leases, asks public buyers to accept a projection, and founder control takes away the vote they would normally use when a projection misses, so the prospectus is where that has to be made up. I'd read two parts of it first. The first is revenue as booked in the quarters the prospectus covers, with adjusted operating income reconciled to its standard version, because every multiple in the pitch depends on which revenue it divides by. The second is every lease and compute commitment in one table, with its term, start date, and total, because those contracts assume the growth lasts for years past the last quarter in the prospectus. If both are easy to find, I think the structure is defensible, since buyers would know what they're giving up and paying for. If either is hard to find, public buyers will have accepted weaker votes without getting the disclosure that was supposed to make up for them.
