# Anthropic's Espionage Report Asks for Trust It Could Have Earned With Evidence

Two weeks after Anthropic said a Chinese state-sponsored group had used Claude to carry out most of an espionage campaign, most of what I've read either treats the report as a landmark or waves it off. The landmark reading follows Anthropic, which in its [November 13 report](https://www.anthropic.com/news/disrupting-AI-espionage) called the campaign the first reported AI-orchestrated cyber espionage campaign. On the skeptical side, [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/anthropic-claims-of-claude-ai-automated-cyberattacks-met-with-doubt/) quoted Daniel Card dismissing it as marketing and Kevin Beaumont arguing that its operational impact should likely be zero because existing detections would catch the open-source tooling, and reading the complete lack of indicators of compromise as a sign Anthropic didn't want to be called out, while Yann LeCun, [replying to Sen. Chris Murphy](https://www.thestack.technology/backlash-over-anthropic-ai-cyberattack-paper-mounts/), cast it as a scare story in service of regulatory capture. Harvard's Fred Heiding, quoted by [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2025/11/19/a-dangerous-tipping-point-ai-hacking-claims-prompt-cybersecurity-debate), landed in between, finding the account broadly credible but "overstated." I think all of those readings skip the question that actually decides this, which is whether anyone outside Anthropic could check the report. For most companies that would be a communications question, but for a company whose brand is safety I'd argue that whether a claim can be checked is part of the product, and by that measure Anthropic was right to publish and wrong about what it left out.

The disclosure is the part Anthropic got right. By its account, it detected the campaign in mid-September and assessed with high confidence that a Chinese state-sponsored group it calls GTG-1002 was behind it, using Claude Code connected to outside tools through the Model Context Protocol, the open standard Anthropic introduced for connecting AI models to other software, against roughly 30 targets across technology companies, financial institutions, chemical manufacturers, and government agencies, with a small number of successful intrusions. Anthropic estimates the AI did 80 to 90 percent of the campaign's tactical work, with humans stepping in at perhaps four to six decision points per hacking campaign, and says the attackers got past its safeguards by breaking the work into innocent-looking tasks and telling Claude it worked for a legitimate cybersecurity firm doing defensive testing. That is a harder problem than the one I flagged in October, when I argued that [moving customization into written procedures was the right bet](/writing/anthropic-agent-skills-written-procedures-platform-bet) as long as the sandbox under it held, because no sandbox can tell a real defender from an attacker who claims to be one. Anthropic banned the accounts and notified the affected organizations, and it volunteered that Claude occasionally hallucinated credentials, an odd detail to include if the goal were pure marketing. A lab that finds its own product used this way and says so in public is doing what I'd want a lab to do.

What the report left out is where it fails. It carries no indicators of compromise and no example prompts. Beaumont may be right that existing detections will catch the open-source tooling, but that only tells a defender they would see generic scanning, not whether this particular campaign touched their network, and a researcher who doubted the report had nothing to test. I came up through QA at Civitech, and I read a threat report the way I'd read a bug report, starting with whether someone else can reproduce it. Nobody reading the public report can reproduce this one, and that matters most for its headline number, since an estimate like 80 to 90 percent of the tactical work is only as good as the logs behind it. It also gives the accused an easy answer. China's embassy, quoted by Al Jazeera, asked for sufficient evidence in place of speculation, and the public report gives nobody a way to supply it. Anthropic argues that defenders need the same capabilities the attackers used, a two-sided shift I [wrote about in January](/writing/cybersecurity-in-age-of-ai-software-engineer-perspective), but defenders can only use a report they can act on.

Anthropic already knows how to publish work other people can check, which makes the gap hard to excuse. On October 6 it open-sourced [Petri](https://www.anthropic.com/research/petri-open-source-auditing), an auditing tool. On November 13, the same day as the espionage report, it released its [political even-handedness evaluation](https://www.anthropic.com/news/political-even-handedness) on GitHub with results showing Claude Opus 4.1 and Sonnet 4.5 slightly behind Gemini 2.5 Pro and Grok 4. And Anthropic's Opus 4.5 launch post on November 24 published how often prompt injection succeeds against its newest model on a benchmark of very strong attacks that the security firm Gray Swan built and ran, which [Simon Willison read](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/24/claude-opus/) as an attack success rate of 4.7 percent with one attempt, 33.6 percent with ten, and 63.0 percent with a hundred. A company that hands out the code to check its political scores and publishes an outside firm's attack success rates against its own flagship model knows how to make a claim checkable, and it didn't apply that standard to the report carrying the most weight.

The missing evidence matters more because of where the report ended up. On November 26 the House Homeland Security Committee's chairman, Andrew Garbarino, and two subcommittee chairs [asked Amodei to testify](https://homeland.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/2025-11-26-CHS-to-Anthropic-re-Request-to-Testify.pdf) at a December 17 hearing, and their letter treats GTG-1002 as a demonstration of what a well-resourced state-sponsored actor can now do with commercially available American AI systems. That is a national security conclusion resting on Anthropic's report and press coverage of it, and a hearing is the committee's chance to test it, but no outside reader can check it before then. Amodei told [60 Minutes](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-warning-of-ai-potential-dangers-60-minutes-transcript/) on November 16 that he is deeply uncomfortable with a few companies and a few people making these decisions about AI, and a threat report only Anthropic can verify is a small version of that problem, since it leaves a congressional committee relying on one company's account of AI-enabled espionage. I think a report's evidence bar should rise with the policy weight it carries, and this one carries a lot.

The best case for Anthropic's restraint is a real one. The worry runs from indicators tipping off the attackers, to prompts handing copycats a working playbook, to an account of how Anthropic caught the campaign teaching the next group what to avoid. The organizations that most needed details were also notified directly. I'd grant that some of it should stay private, but there's a lot of room between publishing everything and publishing nothing.

The standard I'd hold any lab to after this is simple enough to write down. A threat report should give an outside reader something they can check, which I'd define as redacted examples of the prompts, indicators published where that's safe and shared with a named body where it isn't, and an outside firm reviewing the attribution and saying in public whether it holds. That costs a lab some control over its own story, and I think a company that wants to be believed on national security should pay it. What November shows me is that Anthropic made its claims about its own models checkable, from Petri to its political scores to its prompt injection rates, and left its claim about a state attacker, the one that reached Congress, as something readers had to take on its word, and I think that order should run the other way.
