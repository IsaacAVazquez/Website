# Anthropic's Red Lines Held Up in March, and Their Narrowness Helped

On March 26 a federal judge granted a preliminary injunction against the Pentagon's supply chain risk designation of Anthropic, and for most of the month before, the coverage I read treated the company's standoff with the Defense Department, which the administration calls the Department of War, as a test of whether it would survive. CNBC's analysis on March 4 [framed the fight as a possible existential business risk](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/04/anthropic-ai-pentagon-defense-business-risk.html). OpenAI's Sam Altman told a Morgan Stanley conference on March 5 that the government should be more powerful than private companies, while Emil Michael, the Pentagon's chief technology officer, told CNBC on March 12 that policy preferences baked into Claude through its constitution, the document of values Anthropic trains the model on, would pollute the defense supply chain. I think the more useful question is what kind of usage policy survives a fight with a government, and the answer March gave is a narrow one.

When the Pentagon pushed for Claude to be available for any lawful use, Dario Amodei wrote on February 26 that Anthropic "cannot in good conscience accede," and the disagreement came down to [two exceptions, mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons](https://www.anthropic.com/news/statement-department-of-war). Those two safeguards are a narrow slice of Anthropic's much longer public usage policy, and they are the part it refused to give up for this customer. Amodei stressed that Anthropic had never objected to particular military operations. The next day President Trump directed federal agencies to stop using Anthropic, with a six-month phase-out, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth moved to designate it a supply chain risk, a label the Pentagon [had made formal by March 5](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/05/its-official-the-pentagon-has-labeled-anthropic-a-supply-chain-risk/). Holding the two lines put Anthropic's Pentagon business on the way out, and I'd still argue the narrowness is part of what let the policy hold up through the month, from consumers who could repeat it, to cloud partners who could see where it ended, to a judge who could weigh it.

## The consumer surge

Claude reached No. 1 among free U.S. App Store apps on February 28, and Anthropic said it was seeing [more than a million sign-ups a day](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/06/claudes-consumer-growth-surge-continues-after-pentagon-deal-debacle/) in the week that followed. The sign-up count can't separate a reaction to the policy's shape from a reaction to the fight itself, since any public refusal of the Pentagon would have drawn attention, so the most I'd claim for the narrowness here is that the disputed part fits in one sentence and is easy to explain to someone else. Sign-ups are Anthropic's own count, and Similarweb estimated Claude's mobile daily active users at 11.3 million on March 2, against 250.5 million for ChatGPT, so I'd count the consumer case as the weakest of the three.

## Microsoft, Google, and AWS

On March 5 Anthropic [published its own reading of the designation](https://www.anthropic.com/news/where-stand-department-war), arguing that the statute the letter cites reaches only Claude used as a direct part of Pentagon contracts, which would leave most customers, and contractors' work unrelated to the Pentagon, outside it. Microsoft said that day its lawyers had concluded Claude could stay available to its customers other than the Department of War, Google and AWS took similar positions on March 6, and on March 9, the day Anthropic sued, Microsoft announced Copilot Cowork, built in close collaboration with Anthropic. That legal reading did most of the work, and it holds however many exceptions the policy had, so I can't pin the partners' decision on the narrowness alone. What I think the narrowness added is that nothing in Anthropic's own position gave a partner a reason to doubt that reading, since a fight over two named uses in one customer's contract was easy to size against the rest of its business.

There's a second reading too. On March 10 Microsoft [filed a motion to submit an amicus brief](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/10/microsoft-says-court-should-temporarily-block-pentagon-ban-anthropic.html) urging a temporary restraining order for existing contracts, saying the designation imposed substantial and wide-ranging costs and risks on companies building on Anthropic's models and that it might have to reconfigure products and contracts. Microsoft also committed on November 18, 2025 to invest up to $5 billion in Anthropic, so it had reasons of its own to stay. The partners stayed, and by Microsoft's own account staying cost something, and I think both are true at once.

## Judge Lin's preliminary injunction

In its first court filing, on March 17, the Pentagon argued that the red lines [make Anthropic an unacceptable national security risk](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/18/dod-says-anthropics-red-lines-make-it-an-unacceptable-risk-to-national-security/). On March 26 Judge Rita Lin [granted a preliminary injunction](https://fortune.com/2026/03/26/us-judge-blocks-anthropic-ban-supply-chain-risk-orwellian-notion/) blocking the designation and the directive to federal agencies, which takes effect after a one-week delay, finding Anthropic likely to show the government retaliated for its speech and acted arbitrarily. Her findings were about retaliation and process, and nothing in the order turns on how the policy was worded, so I'd count the ruling as evidence that the government's response was likely unlawful, which is a separate question from the narrowness. What the narrowness did give the court was a concrete question, whether declining two named uses makes a domestic vendor a supply chain risk. The order doesn't require the Pentagon to use Claude, and Michael said on March 27 that a separate designation outside her jurisdiction keeps the ban in place, so I'd treat it as a first ruling.

## What holding the line cost

The agreement at stake, signed in July 2025, had a $200 million ceiling. Anthropic's March 9 complaint said hundreds of millions in near-term business were at risk, and at a March 24 hearing it said it could lose billions, though both are litigation claims. Reuters reported that Lockheed Martin was expected to remove Claude, and J2 Ventures told CNBC that 10 of its portfolio companies were replacing it for defense work. The strongest objection, underneath Altman's and Michael's comments, is that choosing suppliers is a sovereign call and a vendor writing military limits into its terms is making the officials' choice. I think that objection has real force, since a military that has to clear uses with its vendor has given the vendor some say over operations. My argument is narrower. Once Anthropic decided to hold a line at all, two exceptions written down in public was the least costly form it could take, because a vaguer policy reserving the right to refuse anything objectionable would have given the Pentagon more to litigate and partners more reason to wonder whether their customers were next, and left consumers nothing specific to repeat. The Pentagon still argued the open-ended version, that Anthropic might disable or alter its model and that its constitution carried policy preferences, but it had to reach past the written exceptions to make that argument.

If March has a lesson for other platforms, it's that a usage policy should be written like a spec, as a short, public list of named uses it won't support, narrow enough that a partner can tell quickly whether it applies to them. In November 2025 I argued that for a company whose brand is safety, [whether a claim can be checked is part of the product](/writing/anthropic-espionage-report-trust-needs-evidence). March carried that logic into policy, and I think being checkable is part of why a policy that cost Anthropic its standing with the Pentagon didn't also cost it the partners that sell Claude.
