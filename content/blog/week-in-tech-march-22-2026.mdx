# The Week AI Stopped Being a Product Story and Became an Infrastructure Fight

If you read the tech timeline this week, most of it was model news, starting with a new Grok. That's the layer the industry loves to talk about because it's legible and it's fun, and I get it. But the two developments I'd actually circle from this week weren't product launches at all. The AI trade wobbled hard for the first time in over a year, and the US government moved to pull Anthropic out of its own stack, which Anthropic is now fighting in federal court. Put those next to each other and I think you can see the story turning. AI is quietly stopping being a question about which product is best and becoming a question about infrastructure, money, and politics, which is a much more serious and much less controllable kind of question.

## The AI trade wobbled for the first time in over a year

The market move is the one I'd start with, because it's the first real crack in a rally that had run more or less one direction for well over a year. On March 13, [a Bloomberg index of the Magnificent Seven](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-13/magnificent-seven-heads-for-a-correction-on-iran-war-ai-risks) closed more than 10% below its October record, which is the usual line for a correction, and Microsoft had fallen further this year than any of the other six. I want to be careful not to overread a few down weeks, because this group has shrugged off scares before and a pullback after a run like that is ordinary. But the character of it is what caught my attention. This wasn't one company missing on one number. It was the market repricing the entire AI complex at once, which is what happens when investors stop asking whether the technology is real and start asking whether the spending on it will ever earn its money back (the question I looked at in [my piece on hyperscaler capex](/writing/hyperscaler-capex-2025-when-does-it-pay-off)). That's a different question, and it's a harder one, because the answer depends on things that won't be settled for a couple of years. The rally treated AI capex as obviously good. The selloff is the first time the market treated it as a bet that could be wrong.

## The government tried to rip out Claude and Anthropic took it to court

The development that I think will matter longer, though, is the fight between the federal government and Anthropic. The government moved to replace its Claude usage within roughly six months and, in the process, labeled Anthropic a supply-chain risk, which is not a normal thing to call a software vendor you've been paying. Anthropic sued on March 9 to overturn the designation, and as I write this no court has ruled on it. Set aside who is right on the merits, because I don't have the filings and I'm not going to pretend to. The thing worth sitting with is the category shift. A frontier model provider is now being treated the way you'd treat a strategic supplier of something like telecom gear or defense components, where the government asserts a national interest in who you can and can't depend on. That's a long way from evaluating a product on features and price. Once a model vendor can be branded a supply-chain risk by its own government, the questions that decide these relationships are trust, control, and standing, and those get argued in courtrooms and procurement offices rather than in benchmarks. I think this is an early instance of a pattern that's going to become common, and whatever the court decides, it won't undo the precedent that someone tried.

## The model firehose kept running, but it stopped being the story

None of this means the product layer went quiet. xAI put out its Grok 4.20 update around the start of the week, leaning into deep real-time integration with X, which is a genuinely interesting distribution advantage even if I'd want to see how it holds up in use. And the integration standard that a lot of this is being built on has been compounding for a while now. [Anthropic said in December](https://www.anthropic.com/news/donating-the-model-context-protocol-and-establishing-of-the-agentic-ai-foundation) that the Model Context Protocol's official SDKs were getting more than 97 million downloads a month across Python and TypeScript, which is a real signal that the plumbing layer is settling into place (I wrote about [the MCP integration layer](/writing/mcp-integration-layer-what-it-means) separately). I don't want to wave any of that away, because a year ago it would have been the whole column. But that's sort of my point. Capability and even distribution have stopped being the scarce things. There's a new capable model most weeks now (my [Frontier Models](/frontier-models) tracker lists them), the integration layer is converging, and the marginal launch changes the standings less and less. What's scarce, and what actually moved this week, is whether the market will keep funding the buildout and whether governments will let any single vendor sit under critical systems.

The pattern I'd pull out of this week is that AI graduated, in the least fun way, from a product you evaluate to infrastructure you fight over. When the market reprices the entire complex in the same stretch that a government tries to eject a leading vendor and the vendor has to sue to stop it, you are not looking at a feature race anymore. You're looking at the early politics of a general-purpose technology, where the questions are who funds it, who controls it, and who is allowed to depend on whom. Those are slower and messier than product questions, and they don't resolve with a better model. I'd rather be tracking them now than be surprised by them later, because I think the next couple of years of this story get written in that register more than in release notes.
